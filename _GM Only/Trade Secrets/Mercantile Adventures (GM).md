## Incorporating Shopping in Your Game

The Shopping downtime activity itself is on the player page [[Shopping & Markets]].

Shopping is one of those roleplaying game activities that players can either love or hate. Shopping trips in RPGs generally involve four stages:

1. The players tell the GM what item or items they want to acquire.
2. The GM tells them whether those items are available and for how much.
3. The players pay that price or haggle for a lower one.
4. The players add the purchased items to their character sheets.

Those steps provide a quick resolution, but they leave little room for introducing more excitement to the procedure. Their main advantage is for players who want an action-oriented approach: you get in, get your stuff, and get back to the dungeon. However, this bare-bones method can leave other players wanting more.

One player’s quest for the perfect item or the best bargain is another player’s waste of session time. Neither perspective is right or wrong, but a GM can help both camps get what they want out of shopping scenarios.

In the spirit of giving every player at the table what they want, consider the narrative and mechanical tools presented here.

### Shopping During Sessions

When players visit merchants and markets, bringing loot to sell or gold to buy new gear, the GM must juggle both the game’s pace and player interest to keep everyone engaged and entertained.

#### Maintaining Pace

Shopping encounters are prone to slowing down a session’s pace, because as soon as a player decides to speak with a shopkeeper, the action switches to a real-time tempo. If this is what everyone at the table wants to do, then no problem! Otherwise, just giving all players equal time in center stage should be sufficient to keep the pace from slackening.

When a shopping encounter does threaten to stall out the game, use one or two of the following tactics to pick up the pace again.

***Fast-Forward Inconsequential Purchases.*** For most purposes, a purchase is “consequential” if the merchant will haggle but still expects to make as much on the item as they paid for it. (As a rule of thumb, assume this is half the item’s listed price.) If an item isn’t particularly consequential, treat the purchase as a simple transaction: pay the cost, and add it to the character sheet.

***Shift Player Focus between Locations.*** If the shopping PCs conclude their business at one shop and immediately set out for another one, tell them their characters are en route.

Then ask the other players what their characters are doing during the shopping trip. Once everyone has a chance to accomplish something, return to the first set of PCs as they enter the next shop.

***1:1 Barter.*** If the settlement’s gp limit isn’t high enough to pay even half of what a PC’s loot is worth (see the **Settlement Economy Limits** table), the GM can rule that the merchant offers their own goods and services equal to the full value of the loot.

For example, a PC who brings in a pair of *boots of elvenkind* can trade them for 1,000 gp of the merchant’s wares, with no coin ever changing hands. A player can do this bit of character sheet business solo, only asking for the GM to check their math and approve the trade.

***Non-Challenging Obstacles.*** If the shopping trip is taking too much table time, introduce a narrative time-consuming obstacle as an excuse to shift focus to non-shoppers. A crowd in the public square makes getting to a stall difficult, or a shopkeeper closed up to run an errand. The obstacle don’t need to be threatening or suspicious, just something that the shoppers must wait out, giving you a natural opportunity to segue to, “And what are you others doing while this is going on?”

#### Keep It Interesting

Hand in hand with maintaining a brisk tempo during shopping excursions, the GM can turn these routine interactions into opportunities to add narrative interest, explore a PC’s backstory (or to further their goals), or just add flavor to the campaign. Towns and cities are hubs of social activities, and a shopping trip is an opening to give roleplayers and explorers more time in the limelight. Try these moments:

***Expand on Character Arcs.*** Use the interactions with NPCs and items to build on the story arcs the GM and the players have in mind for the PCs. A merchant might have met someone from a PC’s backstory, or perhaps they sold an item to the PC’s rival. Something the shopkeeper purchased long ago might have once belonged to someone important to the PC.

Don’t overdesign this. Give a little information, and maybe a tough choice to make—but don’t start a campaign-derailing side quest. Let a small note just happen and move on.

***Introduce New NPCs.*** Shopkeepers have a wider clientele than just adventurers. Locals and other citizens are buying their first crossbow, trying out a new backpack for a journey across the hills, or unloading that silver statuette of a raven they inherited. These background characters often don’t interact with the PCs, but flesh them out a bit and add narrative interest to the shopping trip.

Give them an interesting character trait or two (such as “stunningly beautiful” or “self-important”), as well as a reason to interact with the PCs. These NPCs can be innocent or sinister. Just don’t go crazy. Add a little flavor into a routine shopping trip, or deliver a single clue or hint to the larger story in the main narrative and you’re done.

***Introduce New Storylines.*** Introducing new NPCs leads to introducing new storylines. This is often gossip from the merchant or other customers, such as when the shopkeeper asks if the PCs are pursuing the bounty for a local criminal. The merchant might have suspended purchasing dungeon loot because the wizard who appraises items hasn’t been seen for a few days.

As another option, a new storyline could start with action, rather than words: a shoplifter plants a stolen item on a PC to retrieve later; a group of thugs demands protection money from the merchant; or maybe the PCs find their favorite shop has just been robbed.

***Merchants as Antagonists.*** Most merchants are friendly and want to make a sale. Some are more interested in their own bottom line than in helping the PCs. Players with charismatic characters appreciate opportunities to put their literal skills to the test.

Treating the merchant as a non-combat antagonist lets a player shine in ways that more combat-oriented PCs usually can’t, and it helps validate choices they made during character creation.

#### Shops with Story Arcs

NPCs can have character arcs that play out in fits and spurts, and a merchant and their shop are no exception. In essence, the PCs periodically catch up with the lives and fortunes of NPCs, getting updates on what’s been going on in town while they’re off adventuring.

These storylines work best when they’re mundane, because an NPC’s personal achievements and setbacks are something the players hear about, without feeling a need to get involved. The story arc of the shop can be as simple as the owner’s ongoing expansion plan or the merchant’s infatuation with a certain customer.

Whatever arc you give the shop, remember: The goal is to make the shopping trip a little more interesting, but not to turn it into a side quest. The story arc is for the PCs to hear about second-hand, not their fate to intervene.

***Shops with Character.*** Complex NPCs for the PCs to interact with are a standard and effective tool. You can get similar narrative mileage out of locations with character: a shop with a hidden back room full of contraband, a market stall where the vendor puts on a flamboyant show when customers make a purchase, or an arcane magic shop where the merchant is never seen except as a series of *magic mouth* spells that answer questions.

Players get invested in locations where there’s something for them to do: a reason to roll dice and see what happens when they succeed or fail. Even if the activity has nothing to do with what they came to buy, players are frequently tempted to spend a little time interacting in a shop that has a challenge or wrinkle.

> [!note] Call the Guards!
> While encounters with merchants rarely end in bloodshed, remember that they *can*. In most campaign worlds, a merchant can call the town watch if things turn ugly or hire a few mercenaries to guard the exits if the store sells magic items, jewels, or precious works of art.
>
> If your players often behave aggressively toward merchants, consider designing the merchant’s shop with troublesome customers in mind: *alarm* cast on the entrances at night; a *wand of binding* tucked under the counter; a *scroll of locate* to find stolen merchandise are good starters.

***Shops with Secrets.*** Sometimes shops have character because they have a secret: it’s a front for an illegal business (smuggling, drugs, gambling, or heretical activity); a cult meets in the cellar; a concealed portal to another plane is in the back; a long-unsolved murder is part of the local gossip.

Don’t feel obliged to make a shop’s secret something sinister. Perhaps the shop has a long-forgotten side room, where valuable merchandise has lain hidden for years. Maybe the shop’s inventory includes a certain magic item that mysteriously moves when no one is watching. Or the shop cat is the brains behind the business and gives the shopkeeper good advice.

Give the players a mystery to solve, and they’ll come back again and again—not just to buy new gear or sell loot, but to search for more clues. Dole out the clues judiciously, though—just enough to keep them visiting.

## Story Hooks and Encounters

This section includes advice for crafting commerce-centric adventures, examples of potential conflicts for PCs to get caught up in, and random tables for those times when the GM needs a ready-made plot on the fly.

Statistics for most of the monsters and creatures referenced in these encounters can be found in *Monster Vault* or *Labyrinth Worldbook*. Monsters not in those books list their source beside their name.

### Building Plots and Schemes

Unlike murder and robbery, commerce crimes are based on systems and therefore hinge on the a few key elements:

***Weakness.*** A monetary system must be exploitable in a way that generates profit. Counterfeiting, for example, relies on the difficulty in identifying fakes. Determine what the system’s weak spot is and decide how your villain takes advantage of it.

***Tools.*** These crimes require a physical way to act on the weakness. Smithing tools are commonly employed for counterfeiting or clipping, for example.

***Skill.*** Hand in hand with the tools used to commit these crimes is the skill to pull it off and avoid detection. Skilled NPC criminals should be lean into INT and CHA modifiers. WIS can help spot when the authorities are closing in, and DEX skills give the criminals a chance to slip away.

***Secrecy.*** Because the penalties for some offenses are severe, a criminal’s activities need to be conducted without witnesses (ensuring that might lead to more extreme crimes). If accomplices are involved, they should be loyal or well paid. At the very least, accomplices must be disposable.

### Villains and Currency Crimes

While many crimes involving currency or counterfeiting are subject to harsh punishment, the field is lucrative for those willing to risk it. Here are several currency crimes, along with sample scoundrels who can commit them.

#### Counterfeiting: The Blackforge Family

A dwarf clan of poor reputation, the Blackforges, have a small, windowless room next to their smithy where they have a small operation to make counterfeit coins. The smiths in the family create coin blanks in the main forge, while their children use a miniature forge in the hidden room to coat them in copper, silver, gold, or platinum. Then the family’s eldest daughter, an accomplished engraver, stamps them with a die.

The whole clan is wary of anyone who shows too much interest in their smithy and watches for signs that anyone is growing suspicious. A successful DC 15 INT (Investigation) check reveals that they are operating some sort of secret side-hustle involving precious metals. If confronted, the Blackforges will take extreme measures to avoid the headsman’s axe.

#### Clipping: Feckless Fadlyn

A traveling peddler, Fadlyn regularly holes up in a private inn’s chamber or a remote cabin. There, he uses set of smithing tools to shave the edges off hundreds of coins, then files the sharp edges down to disguise his handiwork. Fadlyn keeps these shavings in small bags concealed in his peddler’s cart.

Spotting a clipped coin requires a successful DC 15 WIS (Perception) check. If he’s found out, Fadlyn does everything he can think of to avoid arrest. Ultimately, Feckless Fadlyn is a coward, and wants to avoid being executed for his crimes. But he’s willing to kill if he thinks it’s his best chance of escaping justice.

#### Forgery: Nomar the Scrivener

Nomar runs a respectable business making copies of deeds, wills, pardons, contracts, and the like. He also has a gambling problem and owes a fortune to a small criminal organization. Although he knows he could lose a hand or an eye if he’s ever caught, the criminals he owes have made it clear that if he doesn’t do as he’s told, he’s in for much worse.

Nomar has been introducing a telltale flourish that he hopes he can use to prove to a judge that he was an unwilling participant, in hopes of a lenient sentence.

Spotting this flourish across several documents is a DC 15 INT (Investigation) check. If he’s caught, Nomar begs to be taken into custody to avoid the wrath of his bosses.

#### Tax Evasion: Lady Lyvylane

A wealthy and unscrupulous noblewoman, Lyvylane has leveraged her education to find loopholes in the kingdom’s tax laws. When tax collectors approach her estate, she moves her livestock to secluded pastureland. She sells various goods or properties at a loss, just to buy them back for only a slightly higher price. She reports things stolen, only to conveniently locate them again a few weeks later. Lyvylane has bricked up the windows on her home (because the tax rate is based on the number of glass windows) and re-glazed them later, and in emergencies, she has ordered her servants to bury entire chests of coins to throw off the tax collector’s tally.

Many of her peers suspect that Lyvylane is evading her taxes and would love to prove it. Given that the punishment is seizure of property (and public humiliation), Lyvylane doesn’t welcome scrutiny . . . though she professes to have nothing to hide.

#### Loans and Usury: Molgor the Cruel

A rotund and jolly man, Molgor seems above reproach. Those who borrow money from him, however, find that his friendly demeanor masks a ruthless nature. Molgor frequently increases his interest rates to 20–30% when a borrower tries to pay him back. He has used his lending contracts to seize property, homes, and to force people into indentured servitude. Even when someone can pay, Molgor often promises to lower their interest rate in exchange for unethical or immoral services, knowing that the truly desperate have little choice.

Molgor has friends among the magistrates though, so anyone who attempts to bring him to justice must have incontrovertible proof of his crimes. Even then, the proof might mysteriously vanish overnight, and anyone speaking against him could find themselves visited by vicious thugs (many of whom are looking for a way to reduce their own debt to their boss).

### Locating & Securing Goods

This section breaks down four types of story hooks involving commerce and trade, presenting them as tables of random quests in four categories: fetch quests, manufacturing quests, smuggling, and protection.

#### Fetch Quests

Each quest on the **Fetch Quests** table sounds simple enough, but comes with complications. Successful crews must think fast and take determined action.

**Fetch Quests**

| d10 | Story Hook |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Transporting Goods.** A trader needs goods delivered to a port city, to be loaded onto a ship. The goods are of average value at their destination, but worth a small fortune at another port. Smugglers want to intercept the shipment and load it onto their ship. |
| 2 | **Transporting Raw Materials.** A manufacturer needs to move a few wagons of silver ore from a mine to a smelter. The owner of the smelter wants the ore without the cost, so he plans to make it look like the ore never arrived. Along with any witnesses. |
| 3 | **Transporting Tools.** Workers need tools. Not having them means work stops cold. A rival pays a group of bandits to reroute the tools to his own workshop by subterfuge, theft, or violence. |
| 4 | **Transporting Livestock.** A rancher’s cattle must go to the slaughterhouse, and the rancher will pay a percentage of each cow that makes it. The route passes through territory prowled by an ankheg that stalks the herd, picking off a few cows every day. |
| 5 | **Transporting Money.** The workers at a remote logging camp need to be paid. That much coin means a big, obvious paychest for bandits to target. The boss wants someone to find a way to get the workers’ pay to them while teaching the bandits a lesson. |
| 6 | **Locating Stolen Goods.** A trader whose caravans keep getting robbed sends a decoy shipment on the same route. The shipment includes a unique art object, and the trader has commissioned several *locate* scrolls to track the robbers to their lair. He expects to recover everything they stole and to turn over the robbers to the law. |
| 7 | **New Trade Route.** No one has ever successfully moved goods along this new route, and a trader offers a reward for anyone who can scout the path and ensure that its major dangers are neutralized or avoided. |
| 8 | **Reopening a Trade Route.** An old caravan trail hasn’t been used for years, but it’s the fastest, cheapest route. A trader offers a reward for anyone who can clear the route of obstructions, monsters, and bandits. |
| 9 | **New Trade Partners.** A trader has made a deal with a supplier far off the usual trade routes and needs to reassure them that their goods will reach market. Some of the supplier’s people object to the deal, and will try to ruin it. |
| 10 | **Transporting a Cursed Item.** Destroying a fiendish statue wasn’t enough to end the curse. The pieces must be returned to an ancient temple, but the curse gets worse the closer the pieces get. |

#### Manufacturing Quests

Ideas on the **Manufacturing Quests** table involve applying crafting skills under difficult circumstances.

**Manufacturing Quests**

| d10 | Story Hook |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Missing Components.** An alchemist has run out of giant’s blood for his most popular potion, *primordial humor* (see *Field Guide* *to Monster Harvesting*). Replacing it means killing at least a dozen true giants and harvesting their blood. |
| 2 | **Quality Components.** A local clothier buys bolts of spider-woven silk from a drow merchant, who tries to fob the clothier off with inferior silk. Recognizing the inferior quality requires a DC 15 INT (Clothier Tools) check, and success motivates the drow to offer the good stuff instead. |
| 3 | **Rush Job.** After a workplace accident, the new granary is only half-finished, and the first grain shipment is soon to arrive. The materials are on-site. Someone just needs to find a way to complete ten DC 20 STR (Construction Tools) checks in only five days. |
| 4 | **Expert Assistance.** An artist is working on a painting of a powerful dragon’s lair. She’s too delicate to travel there and paint *in situ*, but she wants to pay someone to make the preliminary sketches: a DC 15 DEX (Artist Tools) check. |
| 5 | **The Right Tools.** After a tunnel collapse, some miners lost both their mining drill and their blacksmith. Now, they’re barely getting by with picks and shovels. Rebuilding the drill is a DC 14 DEX (Smithing Tools) check, but the components are buried under a few tons of unstable rubble. |
| 6 | **Incentives and Motivation.** The workers at a logging camp haven’t been meeting their quotas, and the boss wants someone to motivate them to work harder by “making examples” of the worst performers. | 7 | **Salvage Operations.** A merchant fleet’s flagship recently sank off the coast, and the captain needs someone to dive down and recover the cargo. The cargo is a hundred feet down, and the wreckage is of great interest to sahuagin raiders. |
| 8 | **Remote On-Site Manufacturing.** After a fire, the master distiller decided to relocate to a group of tents at the edge of the rye fields. The distiller needs workers to malt, mill, and mash the grain, but some local fey are wreaking havoc on the operation. |
| 9 | **Industrial Espionage.** A local orchard’s famous cider recipe has fallen into the hands of their competitor. The apple farmer wants someone to find out which of her workers betrayed her, little suspecting that her halfling cider maker is actually a doppelganger. |
| 10 | **Sabotage.** Someone at the mill has been hindering operations: throwing broken glass into the millstone, smuggling rats into the granary, and setting small fires. The culprit is an imp, who claims that the miller owes it for helping get the mill started, years ago. |

The story hooks on the **Protection Quests** table present suggestions for mercenary work protecting goods moving through dangerous or untamed areas.

|---|---|

| 7 | **Night Watch.** Standing watch overnight pays slightly better, because it’s when the caravan is in the most danger. False alarms are common, but in the pre-dawn hours, brigands approach the camp and demand a “toll” for safe passage. |

### Shopping Encounters

When the PCs dive into the world of civilized commerce, they might find the waters of mercantilism a bit murkier than they’re used to. These encounters are designed to give GMs a jumping-off point for adventures in exchanging coin for goods and services.

#### Shopping Encounters by Settlement Size

Encounters in the **Village Shopping Encounters**, **Town Shopping Encounters**, and **City Shopping Encounters** tables mainly involve Humanoids, encountered in Humanoid settlements, and their plots, schemes, and foibles.

**Village Shopping Encounters**

| d8 | Encounter |
|---|---|
| 1 | **Highway Robbery.** The only merchant in the village dislikes adventurers and adds a 10% surcharge to anything you buy. Convincing her to give you the benefit of the doubt requires a successful DC 16 CHA (Persuasion) check. You have advantage on this check if you can win the support of at least three other villagers—a DC 12 CHA (Persuasion) check. If you fail the check, mistreat anyone in the village, or use magical means to influence her (such as *charm*), she increases the surcharge to 50%. |
| 2 | **Unpopular Merchant.** The village merchant has been an outsider since he arrived and set up shop a few years ago. Rumors claim that he’s a shapechanger who devours villagers at night, though no one can say exactly when or where this happens. The merchant’s business is going under, and he wants you to help him buy a wagon so he can pack up and start again somewhere else. |
| 3 | **Critical Shortages.** Certain items are currently much harder to come by. Anything over 10 gp is double the usual price, and anything over 25 gp is not available in the village. You can try buying those items from a local. With a successful DC 15 CHA (Persuasion) check, an NPC offers to sell their property at twice the usual price. You have disadvantage on this check if the item you want is consumable. |
| 4 | **Buying or Selling.** Your recent magic item acquisition is particularly valuable, and the merchant can’t resist making an offer: 50% of the item’s list value. Assuming you don’t sell, he raises his offer by 5% each visit, until he reaches 125% of its value, or you decide to let the item go. By then, though, the merchant has become bitter about your stubbornness and treats you a little less civilly from that point on. |
| 5 | **Cursed Item.** The village merchant recently bought a box of old junk, which included a *+1 battleaxe*. Knowing that you’re always looking for better gear, he offers it to you, not realizing that it’s a *berserker axe*. Once the weapon’s curse is discovered, the merchant is apologetic! But he’s already put that 1,010 gp into a house and shop in a major city and can’t afford to give a refund. If you insist, he offers a 20% share in his new shop, as compensation. |
| 6 | **Reserved Item.** The village merchant has a valuable magic item proudly on display—one you could really use—but it’s not for sale. The item is reserved by a wealthy noblewoman, and while she hasn’t yet paid for it, she’ll be irate if it’s not waiting when she arrives. You can attempt a DC 16 CHA (Persuasion) check to convince the merchant to sell it to you instead, but even if they succeed, he wants 10% over the list price, because he needs some way to mollify the noblewoman. |
| 7 | **Vermin in the Cellar.** A merchant offers a reward for killing the rats who keep breaking into his cellar inventory. If you accept, you learn the “rats” are two young goblin orphans hiding in the cellar because there’s food and warmth. When the merchant finds out, he wants the children to pay for what they stole (about 10 gp worth of food and drink, which they don’t have), or he'll turn them over to the town guard. What will you do? |
| 8 | **Hostile Customers.** Local outlaws are helping themselves to the merchant’s wares, always “putting it on their tab.” The merchant and others are afraid to cross them, and don’t even want you to do anything about it, lest the outlaws’ friends harm them after you move on. Do you want to turn a blind eye or start a war with a half-dozen well-armed outlaws? |

## Market Events

The descriptions of markets, auctions, the black market, fairs, and festivals are on the player page [[Shopping & Markets]]. The events below are for the GM.

This section contains several events and scenarios for nonstandard business, from the mundane to the magical. Use these as written or as jumping-off points for designing your own unique business encounters.

#### Public Auction Events

***Here are some events you can build on for characters nosing around public auctions.***

***Estate Sale.*** A local of moderate means has passed without any heirs, and his possessions are on the auction block. They’re mostly mundane items—a little jewelry, some fancy clothing, a wagon, a couple of horses—but among the lots is a magic item that one PC can use.

***Eviction Auction.*** A merchant who sold the PCs useful consumable items is being evicted from his shop for failure to pay fines, and the magistrate ordered his goods be sold at auction. Do the PCs participate in the auction (and buy these items at one-tenth their list price)? Do they pay off the merchant’s fines and make a friend for life?

***Impounded Goods.*** Recently arrested criminals keep a wide range of valuables, from expensive jewelry to stables of horses, which are seized and auctioned off by the authorities. Even homes and magic items could be on the block. The PCs have an opportunity to purchase the loot the criminals left behind. But what happens when the original owners come for it?

***Consumable Goods.*** After rumors of war touched off a crafting boom, the kingdom finds itself with hundreds of bottles of common and uncommon potions. An auction has been decreed to recoup some of the cost, and bidding starts at 25% of each potion’s list price . . . in lots of ten vials each.

***Surplus Goods.*** The merchants’ guild has acquired an enormous shipment of magical ingredients and authorized an auction to sell what they can’t store. This is the PCs’ chance to pick up a lifetime supply of spell components for next to nothing.

***Unclaimed Goods.*** The docks are overloaded with crates and barrels unloaded from a merchant ship, never claimed by their buyer. After their collective weight causes a small section of the pier to collapse, the harbor master holds an auction to sell it all to the highest bidders. The goods are mostly semi-valuable commodities like wine, spices, and cloth, but a few items in the mix are just right for the PCs. They’re so good, that others want them too. The bidding gets fierce.

#### Private Auction Events

***These higher-stakes events can occur within private auctions.***

***Wealthy Estate Sale.*** A local merchant of considerable wealth has passed without any heirs, and her possessions are on the auction block. This includes lots of valuable jewelry and objects of art, but also some very rare magic items the PCs can use, at up to 50% off list price. To participate in the auction though, the PCs need invitations.

***Stolen Goods.*** An exclusive auction is being held at the estate of an aristocrat with ties to the criminal underworld. There are magic items the PCs can use, but also items stolen over the past few years, for which there are rewards. Can the PCs secure those goods and get the rewards from the original owners?

***Artifacts.*** The university is auctioning off old items from their vault to make room for new magical curiosities. These arcane items are mostly curios, but among them is an *iron flask* (see *Player’s Guide*). The flask is supposed to be empty, but is it?

#### Public Market Events

Use these events to get your PCs into interesting trouble.

***Run on the Market.*** Rumors that a certain commodity is running scarce have created a round of panic-buying.

Something is either no longer available or the price has doubled. If the PCs are willing to wait a couple of weeks, prices should return to normal.

***Inclement Weather.*** The clouds have darkened and the winds picked up. Most of the merchants are folding their tents, planning to return the next time they’re in town (see the **Marketplace Availability by Rarity** table for when that might be). A successful DC 15 CHA (Persuasion) check convinces the merchant to make one last sale, and the PC has advantage if they know exactly what they want.

***Mob Violence.*** The locals are up in arms about something, and the town square erupts with violence: guards trying to maintain order, and citizens demanding justice. Some people are trying to escape the chaos, but others are looting stalls on their way out of the square. The PCs can try to stop them or take advantage of the moment to help themselves.

***Tax Collectors.*** The local tax assessor is in the market, and anything that the merchants sell is subject to unusual taxation. The merchants aren’t too happy about it. Worse, any loot the PCs sell is subject to a 10% tax for the rest of the day.

***Stop, Thief!*** A ne’er-do-well has snatched a valuable bauble from a merchant’s table and is making a run for it. The PCs can give chase and claim a small reward or turn a blind eye and have the thief in their debt when they meet him later at a tavern or guildhall.

#### Black Market Events

Skullduggery is part of the fun when you’re a professional adventurer.

***A Piece of the Action.*** After the PCs sell an item or two in the black market, a few thugs and their boss come to offer a bargain: Cut the criminals in for 25% of everything they sell in the area, and their names need not be mentioned to the watch. The crime boss clarifies that this is a friendly offer, made in hopes of both parties profiting from future business dealings together. If the PCs refuse, the watch comes to arrest them.

***Betrayal.*** The PCs find a merchant who is willing to trade in contraband and doesn’t ask questions. However, the town guard comes around asking that merchant about the sale. To get some leniency, he names the PCs as accomplices. Now, the PCs are actively sought by the guards, and they face harsh punishment if apprehended (depending on what the PCs bought or sold and how it was originally acquired).

***Playing Both Sides.*** The PCs work with a local merchant who is their go-between with the local criminals. She procures items the PCs want and sells the PCs’ ill-gotten booty without mentioning their names. However, she also quietly takes a cut, and the criminals are starting to wonder who her silent partners are.

#### Fair Events

The activities at a fair that can involve the PCs are manifold.

***Local Unrest.*** The fair is so big that the local magistrate has determined that the town’s merchants must set up their own stalls in their usual location in the public square, far from the crowds at the fairground. Angered at losing out on the revenue, some of the locals seek to sabotage their temporary competitors. Tempers are flaring on both sides. The PCs can help keep the unrest from coming to violence, or perhaps even convince the magistrate to reconsider the arrangement to make the fair fairer for everyone.

***Junk Peddler.*** A kindly old peddler woman sells junk from her pushcart, making up stories about who owned any given item before, and how it brought them luck or wealth or true love. She’s harmless, but when one of her customers encounters their childhood sweetheart after buying a love charm, and another miraculously survives being dragged by a runaway horse, the fairgoers conclude that the next charm anyone buys will make them fabulously wealthy. They mob her, trying to buy it, or all the charms, or the whole cart! The rush turns into a riot, and the PCs might be the only ones interested in ensuring that the little old lady isn’t hurt. To show her gratitude, she gives them her peddler’s cart.

***Pickpockets.*** A few entertainers also work as pickpockets. They’ve been working the crowds for weeks in each new town and village. They use loud, flashy performances (fire-breathing, mimicry) to distract their targets, giving them advantage on their DEX (Sleight of Hand) checks against a target’s passive Perception. They also work in groups, passing stolen goods to one another and use an accomplice pretending to be drunk to avoid being caught in a pat-down. If they are caught with stolen goods, they run and try to lose pursuit in the crowd.

***Sickness in the Air.*** Someone picked up an ague in the last venue, and they only *think* they’re over it. They’re back at work, cooking a popular stew dish that fairgoers enjoy. When people start coming down with fevers and vomiting, rumors spread that the fair is cursed. The available healers are overwhelmed, but the kind cook at the stew pavilion offers to feed anyone who helps out. The PCs must identify that very cook as the source of the illness.

***Trouble Brewing.*** A visiting brewer has made a batch of beer so potent that a single mug can knock out the average commoner. Each mug after the first requires a CON save, with a DC starting at 8 and increasing by 2 with each successive mug. On a failed save, the imbiber feels a sense of euphoria and invincibility . . . and acquires one level of the exhaustion. Each failed save afterward adds another level of exhaustion. Someone’s going to get killed at this rate.

However, no one quite realizes the danger they’re in, and the potent brew is so popular that the next drinking contest will involve quaffing mugs of the beer. When the PCs realize the effect the brew is having, they must decide whether and how to warn their fellow contestants. Will they be believed?

#### Festival Events

Events that can occur at fairs can also occur at religious festivals. Use the previous section to develop scenarios for festivals, and add an element of the divine to lend them atmosphere.

***Spirit of Generosity.*** The beggars on the festival grounds are members of a religious order asking for donations to help clothe and feed the poor. Most passers-by give them a copper or two, but their god’s eye is upon them during the festival. Anyone who gives 10% of their wealth is blessed (as the spell), and has advantage on all CHA checks until the festival ends.

***Guest of Honor.*** The local priest asks one of the PCs with the ability to wield Divine magic to offer a homily at the next big service during the festival. The PC is expected to speak for a few minutes, making a DC 12 CHA (Persuasion) check at the end. If this check succeeds, the attendees are generous with donations. If the check fails, the attendees are unmoved. If the PC rolls a 1 on this check, the attendees are deeply offended. They demand that the PC issue a heartfelt apology (requiring a fresh successful DC 15 CHA (Persuasion) check) and then leave town.

***Ecstatic Vision.*** On the eve of the festival, a merchant announces that she has been visited by the holy figure being celebrated: an angel, saint, martyr, or the god in person. The figure appeared as a Small being of pure light, and told her to dedicate her life to serving the weak and infirm. Her account is dismissed at first, but the same thing happens again the next night to a butcher. His mission is now to feed the hungry and homeless. More sightings happen (mostly self-serving fabrications), until the PCs see the figure for themselves. A fey guardian is using its Change Shape trait to appear to the well-off, on behalf of the guardian’s ward: a dying beggar who wants to improve life for his friends.

***Hand to God.*** The festival’s administrators rent holy relics from their collection to the temples they visit. The temple clergy find that new relics attract pilgrims, and the festival keeps the relics in rotation. This year, they brought a mummified hand of a priestess known for her healing gifts. Perhaps it could heal some afflictions of the locals! Shortly after they deliver it to a local clergyman, the same local clergyman arrives to collect the hand. A charlatan disguised as the priest now has a holy relic, and the festival and temple ask for the PCs’ help in recovering the relic.

***Pickpocket!*** Several festivalgoers have found that some of their possessions have gone missing, from their coin purses to small jewelry to items as mundane as buttons, feathers, and hat pins. Because of the randomness of the items stolen—and how no one has spotted a possible suspect in the vicinity at the time—the consensus is that it’s mass hysteria. But the items are still missing, and the truth is that an imp has been stealing these things to torment the religious folk on their sacred holiday.

***Faith Healing.*** This year, the festival brings a living miracle: a teenaged girl whose touch cures sickness, whose tears cure wounds, and whose blood lifts curses. Though the local clergy are skeptical, the girl passes every test they put to her, even revivifying a young man who was crushed by an overturned wagon with a kiss. Her abilities are real, but she’s also unhappy with this role in life. She sees her gifts as the only curse she can’t lift. If the PCs can devise a way to smuggle her away from the festival (without blame falling on them, and definitely without anyone thinking to come looking for her), she promises to make her gifts available to them upon request.

### Magical Events

Not every shop is as mundane as a market stall or a storefront in the merchant district. From time to time, markets appear from beyond the Material Plane, trading in items that mere mortals can’t comprehend.

#### The Tesseract Transactional

A powerful Fiend, Tanark the Bargainer, spent eons establishing her interplanar bazaar, the Tesseract Transactional. The market is vast, with thousands of merchants from many worlds across the Labyrinth, created for the Infernals to rival the Smithy, run by the Keepers of the Keys.

The bazaar “features” fewer wards and less order than the Smithy, selling nearly everything imaginable and some things that mortals are better off without. Tanark’s power in her bazaar is so absolute that even ancient enemies hesitate to quarrel, for fear of banishment from the market-realm.

The bazaar is large, complex, and chaotic; finding a particular shop has been the basis of years-long expeditions. Many who came here out of curiosity have since given up on returning and made the Tesseract their home. However, the bazaar contains multitudes of doorways between worlds, making it not just a marketplace at a crossroads, but a shortcut in itself. Though it’s best to have a clear map of how to get from Gate A to Gate B.

A distinct feature of the Tesseract is the large number of meeting houses that only passingly contain a shop. Individuals of an ilk can buy a membership (at a steep cost) and enjoy a certain fraternity with those of like mind. The best information is found in these places.

Because these gateways exist on the Material Plane, the bazaar can become an adventure location: a place where the PCs can buy a forbidden legendary magic item that isn’t found in more lawful marketplaces.

##### Tesseract Businesses

Here are some places to find what you need in the Tesseract.

***Esteemed Teahouse of the Celestial Voice.*** An alchemist’s restaurant, serving tea concoctions steeped in boiling water to mimic the effects of various powerful potions.

***The Palace of Forbidden Delights.*** A bordello that traffics in souls, rather than flesh. Frequented by fiends and particularly powerful mortals, the Palace is infamous for being the place where living things can be purchased.

***Elements of Style.*** A franchise of four distinct and individual shops, each operated by elementals of air, earth, fire, or water. Each sells clothing themed (and offering immunity to) its particular element.

***The Afterworld Parlor.*** Run by a powerful lich, the parlor caters exclusively to the dead. It’s filled with spirits, all drawn together to speak and share secrets with others of their ilk from across eons of unlife.

***Patrons’ Place.*** A meeting place for supernatural entities who traffic in Wyrd magic. They can make trades, hash out disagreements, or simply talk shop.

***Stars and Voids Tavern.*** Here’s a relatively calm place for visitors to have a meal and a drink they can digest, and a little entertainment that won’t drive them mad. More powerful extraplanar travelers also slum it with the mortal tourists. Conveniently, it has a portal to the Astral Plane in the common room, but the key for it might cost you.

***The Auberge Oneirique.*** A reasonably safe place for visitors to sleep, with rooms overlooking the Ethereal Plane. From time to time, however, guests vanish, along with their entire room, becoming ghosts or other incorporeal Undead.

#### The Tormentum

Collectors and aficionados of arcane artifacts sometimes speak of a mysterious shop, full of legendary riches and wonders lost to time, that appears on nights of the full moon, and never in the same place twice. The shop is home to a figure out of myth: Stonna the Lost, who out-haggled with a god over a priceless treasure, only to lose everything he held dear—family, friends, homeland, even his beloved pet dog.

Stonna’s shop is unstuck in the Labyrinth, drifting through all possible worlds, stopping only for a few nights in one before being drifting to the next location. Stonna’s curse is that he can never leave his shop. However, at each stop he invites customers to browse his wares, which include legendary and fabled magic items, as well as items outside of most mortal comprehension.

Stonna sells items in exchange for platinum coins, the currency of his homeland. He sometimes trades his most valuable stock for artifacts of his prior life: a locket bearing the image of his wife, for example, or a wash basin from his childhood home. Stonna believes he can regain what he lost by trading for it, and that every new place he visits brings him closer to ending his curse. He suspects that each location is not random, but instead is bringing him closer to something he lost.

Because Stonna can’t leave his shop, the Tormentum might seem like a ripe target for a bit of shoplifting. Stonna doesn’t resist, knowing that anyone who takes something from his shop without paying for it suffers a curse: a driving obsession to return the stolen item, to the exclusion of any other concern, no matter how long it takes. Stonna’s curse also ensures that no one can kill him or destroy his shop. Attempts to do so seem to succeed, but the Tormentum is back the next full moon, and Stonna is once more behind the counter.

The GM can present Stonna to deliver powerful magic items that the PCs need to complete a major quest. This would be an artifact from the hoard that Stonna won from the god who cursed him. For something like this, Stonna expects something truly exceptional in trade, such as his wife’s wedding gown, a drawing his daughter made, or his dog’s collar. The PCs face a logistical dilemma: find and acquire what Stonna wants and return before the last night of the full moon (when the shop vanishes again), or acquire the item and then try to find the Tormentum again.

Of course, the items that Stonna seeks are not easily procured. They were separated from the cursed merchant by a god, after all. An item like Stonna’s drinking mug could be the source of an epic quest, with divine entities and horrific monsters challenging the search.

#### Kakofonia’s Collection of Mortal Measure

Kakofonia is a creature of the supernatural, neither good nor evil, more akin to a warlock’s patron. She wanders from place to place, ingratiating herself with powerful figures, and providing advice in exchange for a place to sell her wares.

Her stock consists of immaterial and conceptual goods such as memories, curses, luck, or clarity. The only currency Kakofonia accepts is favors, the most basic of which is replenishing her stock of a given item. Buyers relinquish a memory, take on a curse, give up luck, or allow Kakofonia to remove a hand or heart, without killing the customer, but not leaving them what you would call well either.

Kakofonia can trades useful tools or information to the PCs in exchange for something they feel they don’t need. She offers no refunds but sells things back in exchange for something of greater value.

**Kakofonia’s Wares**

## Investment Opportunities

The investing rules are on the player page [[Running a Business]]. Use your imagination in devising new opportunities and the circumstances under which those opportunities present themselves. Some sample investment scenarios and potential complications are provided in the **Investment Opportunities** table.
