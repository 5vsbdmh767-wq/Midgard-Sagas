## Flavors of Horror

The book covers seven subgenres, each with reading, film, and game lists. They mix freely.

| Subgenre | What It Leans On |
|---|---|
| Body horror | Violation, mutilation, or transformation of the body by an outside force, dwelt on in detail to evoke disgust and helplessness |
| Cosmic horror | Things beyond human understanding, existential dread, humanity's insignificance; less gore, more dread |
| Dark fantasy | Fantasy and horror tropes blended, with higher consequences and personal stakes |
| Folk horror | Folklore and superstition in isolated or rural communities, often with outsiders as protagonists |
| Gothic horror | Crumbling buildings, the weight of history, hauntings, dreamlike or nightmarish moods |
| Monster horror | Surviving a particular creature, in one event or a series of encounters |
| Paranormal horror | Ghosts, poltergeists, and the angry dead who can't rest |

## Establishing Atmosphere

### Pacing

Vary it. Constant pressure exhausts players. Look at how horror books, films, and games in the same subgenre escalate tension, when they break it with levity, and when they linger on description versus rush past it.

### Tone

Your word choice and delivery set the mood more than anything else. Match description to the subgenre: body horror is graphic, Gothic horror is ornate and keeps distance. Build suspense by revealing a monster a piece at a time.

The book stresses hope against fear. If every attempt fails and the villain seems unbeatable, players dissociate or burn out. Give glimpses of hope and real chances to win.

### Set Dressing

The book's suggestions, all optional and subject to the table's accessibility needs: themed art and decor, fewer distractions and planned breaks, dim lighting or colored LEDs, background music and ambient sound at a low volume, and simple props such as handwritten letters or tea-stained maps.

## Crafting Villains

### Elements of a Horror Villain

The book names four:

| Element | What It Means |
|---|---|
| Control and helplessness | The villain is always in control, even when the PCs gain ground. It decides when a confrontation ends and engineers every meeting from a position of strength. |
| Motivation | Know why the villain acts and what it ultimately wants, even for an atypical villain such as a haunted house. It makes improvising easier. |
| Maintaining mystery | Keep the players in the dark and let the truth come out slowly. |
| Unknowable horrors | In cosmic or folk horror, the full truth may never be revealed, and the unanswered questions linger. |

### Monster Theory

The book adapts the seven theses from Jeffrey Jerome Cohen's *Monster Theory* (1996) as prompts:

1. The monster reflects a culture's fears.
2. The monster always escapes and returns, reshaped.
3. The monster defies categories.
4. The monster lives at the gates of difference.
5. The monster polices borders.
6. Fear of the monster is a kind of desire.
7. The monster stands at the threshold of becoming.

Each thesis comes with questions to ask about your world and the PCs.

## Running Villains by Tier

| Tier | Advice |
|---|---|
| 1 (levels 1 to 4) | Keep goals small and level the PCs quickly. Build the villain slowly through rumors, evidence, and victims so it doesn't become a pushover. |
| 2 (levels 5 to 10) | Use the same slow build, and put minions in front of the villain as threshold bosses. Each one's defeat leads to the next and hints at the villain. |
| 3 (levels 11 to 16) | Give the PCs recurring contact with the villain so it feels personal. Build an escape route into the villain's own plan. Size the final fight with the *Monster Vault* benchmark: add the PCs' levels, multiply by ¾, round down, and find that total on the villain's Benchmark Challenges table. |
| 4 (levels 17 to 20) | Use every tool from the earlier tiers, with foes as capable as the PCs. Study the villain's stat block until it clicks. |

The party is 4th level (bible v5.26), so tier 2 villains are the next fit.
