The first thing to understand about the [[The Shadow Realm|Shadow Realm]] is not its people but its land. In the mortal world, ground, forests, and mountains are permanent, and light and shadow come and go. In the Shadow Realm it is the other way around: shadows are eternal, and land and rivers are far less certain.

In the lands of the shadow fey and bearfolk, the sable elves and wyrd gnomes, the forests walk and the mountains wander. It happens too slowly for most to see, but over days and weeks, when the tides shift, the land shoves rivers and even cities away from their usual places. These ripples in reality are the ebon tides, the stretchings and squeezings of the land that shadow folk know and plan for. A survey map is good for a season, or sometimes for a lifetime, if the lifetime isn't too long. Hills crumble under eroding moonlight and wash out to sea. Harbors silt up. Forests quiver and rearrange themselves around riverbeds that wander back and forth like liquid clocks. The realm drives cartographers a bit mad, and guarantees them steady work.

Not everything moves. Landmarks, great cities, headlands, and elder rivers hold true to one another, bound first by the [[Shadow Roads|shadow roads]] and then by all three tools of oak, road, and stone. Some ancient roads shift one grain of sand a century. Some towers keep a forest in their orbit. Rangers, wanderers, wise women, and the unerring elves know their way from place to place. For everyone else, and for anyone foolish enough to anger a geomancer, the plane and its movements are a constant struggle.

## Drifting Places

Some places are anchored. Others come loose.

The most famous unmoored place is [[Oshragora]], the city of psychic vampires and dark deeds. It is said to have once been a kingdom of wizards, or a land so overrun with magic that it became a magical wasteland; either way, its corruption and reckless magic dragged it whole into the Shadow Realm. It has never had a road to anywhere else (its vampires seem unable to build one), and they have never laid foundation stones or planted rooted trees to hold it down. So the city drifts across the land, sometimes near the forests of the shadow fey, sometimes near the town of [[Court of Night and Magic|Wormwood]], sometimes near the darakhul pits. Some say this suits the vampires, since it always brings them somewhere new with fresh victims. Void cults and the satarre have tried to bind Oshragora to the wider realm with a road, but the fey or the bearfolk have stopped every attempt.

Courts drift too. The Court of Wine and Roses was built before anyone understood foundation stones; it seems tangled in the southern forests, and isn't hard to find if you know roughly where to look. The Lost Court of the Triple Moon, said to have had a luminous tower that outshone the true moons and stars, hasn't been seen in centuries and was probably unmade by the Lord of the Hunt or one of the darker fey lords. Maps that claim to lead to lost courts are a common grift among the less scrupulous shadow goblins and shadow fey.

Sometimes a single black oak, a cherry orchard, or a stand of birches drifts loose and takes the nearby homes with it, becoming a wandering village. Most such villages need a new road to bind them back to their neighbors and restore trade, but some thrive without the burden of gifts to the fey or tolls to the darakhul. A few of these freeholds are well known and turn up every few years on the plains or as a river island: the fishing village of Willow Song, the woodworkers' hamlet of Rowan's Heart, the cheesemakers' hilltop of Perreville, the druids' retreat of Owlbridge, and the shepherds' village of Cloverfield. They are never on any map, because they never connect to a road.

## Oak, Road, and Stone

The peoples of the realm use three enchanted tools to keep their lands steady and to build realms that can stand against the ebon tides. Natives swear by them ("By oak, road, and stone!") because they know how much depends on them. Destroying any of the three is at best an act of chaos, since it unravels the habitable lands. Cutting roads, felling more timber than the druids allow, and defacing foundation stones are crimes among all the civilized peoples, though the more chaotic fey treat them as sport.

### Oak

The forests [[Hecate]] first raised, and that the shadow fey, bearfolk, and lately the darakhul have planted since, hold the land together so that it can be farmed, built on, and hunted. Forests don't bind places the way roads do. They work more like a mat of reeds or kelp, keeping a large area calm and moving it as one piece. A great forest floats on the ebon tides like an island, carrying its trees, courts, castles, villages, and streams with it. For this reason even the darakhul plant their strange, crackling forests.

### Road

Roads bind places to one another so that they don't drift apart and end up with a sea between them. See [[Shadow Roads]].

### Stone

Foundation stones are glyph-marked, enchanted stone pilings that tie a structure to its place, so that huts, castles, and palaces don't move when the land is pulled one way or another. A hut, tavern, or small barn needs one stone under the floor; a large inn, manor, or keep needs two or four at the corners; castles with many towers, large monasteries, cloisters, and arcane colleges need more. When a foundation stone breaks, everyone inside feels the building shudder for a moment, like a small earthquake.

## Signs of the Tides

Natives know the signs that the ebon tides are running, whether it's a creek shifting its bed, a whole grassland on the move, or a village that has come loose from its road. Common ones include strange chatter in the wind and voices just out of hearing; the sound of water far from any river; ghostly echoes of the past, forests and even cities appearing and vanishing; a black wind like thick fog; rolling earth, as if the ground were tilting; light and shadow quivering as though someone were shaking the moon; a sound in the sky like half a crashing wave and half a great gong; shadows thickening until the ground is hard going; a new radiant well opening and throwing a beam of light into the sky; and a blaze of light rolling through like a wave, blinding for a moment.

Natives speak of "high tide" travel, when the roads stretch and distances grow, and "low tide" travel, when distances shrink and travelers arrive early. Neither is thought unusual. Along the shadow roads the effects are much milder than overland.

The best-loved maps of the realm are tidal maps, enchanted to show the land both at High Tide, at its most spread out, and at Low Tide, when places crowd together, and to show which state holds now. The fey think of them as fairly ordinary objects, useful but nothing to boast of. Visitors consider them essential.

## Sky and Waters

There is never a true noon in the Shadow Realm, nor proper daylight, though a muddy light is common. A truly black night is almost impossible too: stars, comets, luminous streams and clouds, and a faint glow from the fog and the land itself always give some light. Colors fade quickly except where magic or firelight touches them, and bright things brought from the mortal world sometimes keep the muted tones of shadow even after they go home.

The realm's streams, lakes, and rivers are more dangerous than its sky. They carry rushing ribbons of pure shadow stuff, especially in spring, when shadow seems to run down the hills with every gray rain. Two great rivers twist like lengths of black silk across the plane, the Styx and the Lethe (see [[The Ever River]]), and their tributaries reach nearly every corner of the realm, for those who can survive the water. The realm's shores look out on the inky Shade Sea, an endless, rolling darkness to the south, and the chill Frost Gulf to the north. Few crews who sail out onto the open Shade Sea return, and those who do are never the same.

## Mirrors and Echoes

From a star's-eye view, the Shadow Realm would look like the mortal world seen through a smoked-glass mirror. Great landmarks have echoes in shadow, sometimes alike in spirit and sometimes the opposite. A mortal city might match a city of umbral schemers, or a desolation of tumbled stones and salted fields. But knowing mortal geography is no key to finding your way. The plane stretches and twists, distances deceive, directions blur, and the relationships between places change over time, the way a shadow lengthens and shrinks through the day. A mountain pass might lead to a great forest one year and a desert of black sand the next.
