==**HENOTHEISTIC** ==
A henotheistic religion has many gods, but groups or individual devotees worship one of those gods. It is the standard assumption in most fantasy game worlds. As such, many groups are accustomed to it, and many settings are built around it.

In Midgard, henotheistic practices tend to be a subset of polytheistic practices. While the populace at large will worship many or all the gods of a particular region, it will be the case that certain families, individuals, temples, or religious orders might instead dedicate themselves to a single divinity. 

==**OVERT**==
An overt pantheon is the most common type in fantasy fiction. It refers to any pantheon that actively and verifiably intervenes in the game world. At its most obvious, this can involve deities walking the earth, actively smiting the damned, and handing blessings to the worthy, where any  passerby you meet might well be a god in disguise. 

An overt pantheon is active in the world. The way the gods interact with their worshippers, each other, and current events defines their worship. 

==**GODS AND MASKS**==
The gods and goddesses of Midgard are not individuals; they are archetypes or incarnations of universal forces. They aren’t people in the same sense that individual mortals are. Though they all arise from the cosmos, and their power comes from the strength and numbers of their priests and followers, the same god can look and act entirely differently from one region to another. The beliefs of the faithful similarly vary from place to place, even among those who purport to worship the same deity. Many gods of Midgard go by multiple names and even switch gender and appearance when it suits them. 

As a result, the gods are ultimately unknowable and mysterious, and their faiths embody shifting channels of power. Savants believe that only five or six gods might truly thrive in a city, and perhaps only three in a town, and one in a village. Shrines devoted to more than a few gods rarely prosper—the priests of these extra-numerary gods find that their ability to heal or to intercede with the faithful through divine magic is limited; their power is constrained to a single domain, their prayers go unheard, and their sacrifices are of no consequence. But the human heart has room for many gods. Faith is not a matter of choosing a single god, but of choosing the right god for a particular need or occasion. In practical terms, this means that pantheist priests are common throughout the world. 

The gods seem determined to hide their true identity from worshipers, and sometimes they pretend to be other gods entirely. This sort of deception is common: [[Horus]] might be [[Wotan]], and [[Khors]] might be [[Aten]]. And, in the North, any fool can see that [[Perun]], [[Mavros]], and [[Thor]] are clearly brothers if not the same being. Although malleable and changeable, each of the gods retains a core identity that the god’s faithful recognize, even in altered shape or color.

==**FIRST GODS**==
The gods have their own goals and needs, and above all they play a game of power and deception among themselves, because gods, once made, can also be unmade. The story begins in the days of the great bridge Bifrost and the World Tree Yggdrasil, when the serpent Ouroboros ([[Veles]]) first made and encircled the realms of mortals. In those days there were no gods. Elves, giants, and other ancient peoples walked the earth, valkyries soared above it, and the dwarves did not yet exist. Mortals fought to survive, invented the spear, gained the gift of fire, and wondered about the world. Those who could speak to the dead, the shamans and druids who mastered soul magic, were the nearest thing to priests.

The Northern gods were once humans and elves, the early druids. One of these priests struck down a dragon, bathed in its blood, and learned secrets of power. Filled with the force of a hundred ley lines, [[Wotan]] climbed the [[Yggdrasil|World Tree]] and grasped the secrets of the runes, and with the help of his fellow priests he became divine. Together they built Valhalla and ruled all the world. These first gods demanded worship, blood, treasure, and the loyalty of lesser creatures, and they were suspicious of anyone who sought to know them too well, for they knew anyone might drink from the wellsprings of power, and they guarded those wellsprings jealously. Some say the wellsprings are the thirty heart scales of the World Serpent. Some say they are the flames of Valhalla, fed with wood from the World Tree. Some say they are older gods and titans, chained and shackled world spirits who gave their strength to the new gods and still live in bitter darkness somewhere in a hidden prison.

Many gods walk the branches of the World Tree, and they keep their true power hidden, for the greatest enemies of the gods are their own kind.

==**SHACKLES AND BETRAYALS**==
The gods did fight and enslave one another. [[Loki]] betrayed the early gods by giving power to a lover in the South, the purring, deceitful [[Bastet]], who became the goddess of cats and perfume, and he befriended the thursir and jotun giants, who taught him lore to make the fire god nearly as strong as Wotan and his runes. Other gods rose by striking down weaker gods, demigods, and nature spirits. The stories and slanders run together, each god accusing the others of murder, cannibalism, and the theft of divine power. In the end, jealous [[Aten]] killed a dozen of the southern gods, Ra first, then Isis, Nut, and [[Ptah]], growing stronger with each death, though some dead gods have returned centuries or millennia later. In time even [[Thoth-Hermes]], Wotan, and mighty [[Veles]] grew afraid that the gods would dwindle to a few survivors, with all the rest slain or enslaved.

==**THE WORDS OF PEACE**==
Veles spoke the Words of Peace, which compelled the gods to stop making open war and to wear masks, so that they might not know one another. The Great Serpent warned Aten that the sun need no longer shine from the South if the slaughter of mortals, demigods, and gods did not end. So the open war of god against god became a quieter struggle, waged so as not to rouse the Great Serpent again. Now oracles, heroes, and demigods fight on the gods' behalf, and the gods meddle in mortal affairs more than ever. If a god cannot hold the sword that slays a rival, it can give its favored weapon to a priest-hero and take pleasure in the hero's deeds.

==**WHY SO FEW TRUE GODS**==
All this mayhem is why so much about the gods is a mystery. Their names shift, and they wear masks to show themselves as they wish to be seen and to avoid being replaced entirely. Sages claim only thirty true gods can exist at any time, only five in any great city and six in a great kingdom. Beyond that, some fall into the ranks of demons and angels, still powerful but unable to rise to the Heavens or rule in [[The Eleven Hells]]. Those who fall will do anything to regain their standing, and their usual price is far more than animal blood: they demand the souls and children of their followers. These are the dark gods, full of promises and terror.

The gods are a shifting family of alliances and betrayals who see mortals as useful tools. Every god clings to the secret of how it became divine, and holds tight to its divinity, for even gods fade, wither, and die. The gods of Midgard are friendless, and yet mortals' greatest allies as well as their greatest foes and betrayers. Above all they face one another, and to most of them mortal voices are faint. All mortals should know this before they walk the path of priest or petitioner.

==**WHY THE GODS WEAR MASKS**==
Philosophers offer three reasons, and divine heralds give their priesthoods variations on them:

1. New names and slightly different avatars help the gods slip into new regions and win new followers without fighting regional or racial prejudice. Surely Ariadne is happy to be called Rava, as long as her goals are met.
2. Masks are insurance. If a horde from the east burns every temple of [[Perun]], but the fanes of [[Thor]] in the North stand untouched, the Thunder God keeps some of his worship and power.
3. The gods use masks for their own purposes and want deniability. A god might use a mask to kill another god behind the scenes and take over its temples and worshipers wholesale. The dead god's worshipers still pray and still receive spells, though over time the god's demands shift toward the goals of the god who took over the faith.
