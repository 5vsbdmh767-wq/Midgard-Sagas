Finance covers economic activity related to money, currency, or accounting. It’s how economic activity is measured and how businesses know whether they are thriving or failing.

Finance is also a line of trade and commerce all by itself: banking, loans, accounting, and investments. This form of business can overshadow all others when economic systems grow in scale and complexity—or when the wealthy take advantage of the poor.

## Methods of Exchange

While currency is finance’s primary concern, trade can occur with other means of exchange, and these can be important in a fantasy economy. In villages and other small communities, currency transactions can be rare to nonexistent. Barter, credit, clan systems, and other forms of transaction can govern commerce instead of coin.

### Barter Systems

Barter is direct trade in goods or services for other goods and services. This can be either direct trade where each party wants what the other party is offering personally, or indirect trade where a merchant trades with several parties to pile up a stock of goods. Barter trades can also be for promises of goods and services delivered over time.

While barter works, it has limitations and challenges. Direct trade requires that traders have immediate use for what they trade. Someone might want an apple, but if that wanter doesn’t have anything the apple owner desires, no deal. This kind of challenge ultimately leads to the development of currency, or at a minimum, a system for IOUs where current goods and services are traded for future goods and services. Delayed transactions can themselves create problems, such as inequitable trades and running disputes over terms.

As the complexity and number of barter transactions grow in a community, the time it takes to barter becomes its own significant cost. Currency tends to reduce the overall cost of commerce, making transactions quicker and more flexible.

### Clan Systems

Clan systems, also called traditional economies, make economic trade largely implicit rather than explicit. These systems are a natural outreach from family structure to the overall community. Community members work for the good of the group and share resources in accordance with tradition and under the guidance of leaders, often its elders.

Social expectations determine what is expected from each member and what each member can expect. For instance, a farming community expects every able-bodied adult and the older children to work during planting and harvest season. Such societies help those who show fealty and follow its rules. Those who participate are taken care of by the community when they are ill, injured, or elderly.

Clan economies rely heavily on social cohesion and mutual trust. When these break down, communities fracture into independent or rival groups. A once-thriving farming village might become poverty stricken, a target for bandits and outlaws.

Traditional economies coexist with others by operating with fealty exchange within the group and using other means of trade with outside groups. Religious orders, military services, and family groups are all good examples. Nomadic herders are often clan-based, measuring wealth in livestock, just as clan-based farmers measure wealth in grain or seed, and fishing clans measure wealth in boats and nets.

### Command Economies

Command economies operate like a mix of clan and barter systems, but they feature a sovereign authority who dictates the terms of trade. The sovereign structures society in a hierarchical power structure—with the sovereign person or family at the top. Feudal systems are a good example of this.

The sovereign determines a set of laws that dictate property rules and terms of trade. Outside of those laws, individuals can make their own economic decisions. Command economies often include barter and currency systems, but the value of currency and the freedom to barter are both subject to sovereign whims and changing fortunes of the sovereign’s family.

Command economies often include barriers to economic prosperity and military power to preserve the existing regime. The bottom rungs of the social ladder own little beyond personal possessions. Serfs, peasants, and lower class artisans are effectively forced to work as directed by the central authority. They rarely have time or opportunity to grow into a better life; their toil is taken by others to serve a baron, king, tyrant, or emperor.

### Honor Systems

Honor systems often develop as an extension of traditional economies in systems where society has adopted more commercial models of trade. Honor systems encourage participants to freely offer goods and services to those in need with the understanding that if their circumstances were reversed, they would offer the same in return. Hospitality traditions are a good example of an honor system. Travelers can expect strangers to provide food and shelter in an honor society. In return, travelers must be good guests and return the hospitality when they are in their own homes.

A classic example is a knight who helps anyone in need at no cost because of their sworn duty to protect the weak. In return, the knight receives food, lodging, and obedience from the population as gratitude. A bad knight would take more than was given by the host. A bad host would feed the knight poor fare or refuse housing for more than a single night.

### Tax and Transfer Systems

Once traditional economies grow larger and societies grow less cohesive, they sometimes try to have the best of both worlds. They attempt to keep certain aspects of communal support while moving to independent economic trade. The knight wants to retain hospitality, but also wants to sell his services to a mercenary company, for instance.

In a tax and transfer system, the state taxes the populace and uses it to distribute goods and currency to those who need financial support. These can be direct payments (such as for poorhouses or orphanages) or they can be paid as wages for state work projects, such as canals, roads, or granaries.

While these aren’t exactly trade, they do involve moving wealth and a type of economic activity. Most merchants know that a tax pays for the bridge they need to get goods to market.

### Warfare

While warfare isn’t commonly understood to be a means of trade, it is often pursued for economic reasons. It certainly results in exchanges of goods, services, and capital between neighboring societies, if only in the form of tribute and plunder.

Nations trade with one another for security when one nation accepts tribute as nation-scale protection money or buys help against a rival.

Some traditional economies develop raiding cultures to gain resources, such as stealing sheep, gold, or iron. In raiding cultures, each group’s relative martial prowess might determine its terms of tribute between clans. The better their reputation, the steeper the price they command. Over time, these exchanges can turn from actual violence to ritual combat and elaborate displays of power, to retain each group’s pride and independence while saving the cost of bloodshed and death.

## Currency

Currency is a standardized medium of exchange for goods, services, and promises thereof. It’s coinage, paper money, and even letters of credit. What sets a currency apart from any other kind of good is its acceptance as valuable within an economy and between widely separated economies.

### What Gives Money Value?

Ultimately, what makes currency valuable is trust. This trust can be distributed through all of society, knowing that others will honor the currency, or it can be anchored by a political or economic power that guarantees the currency’s value with its power and wealth.

Some currencies have their own intrinsic value. Gold pieces and silver pieces retain value even when melted down and used for other purposes. Some cultures have used staple crops as currency, at least until eaten.

Currencies can also be intrinsically cheap. Paper notes or common metal coins (bronze or nickel) work well, as long as trust stays intact. Generally, the issuer of a cheap currency guarantees that if you bring the currency to them, you can get a valuable backing commodity in exchange. This creates trust and confidence in the value of the currency. No matter what happens, it is at least as valuable as the commodity you can trade it for.

Fantasy currency can go beyond metals or foodstuffs. Magical coins might be backed by arcane power; vampiric coins might be redeemed for blood. Souls, memories, or divine favor might back a planar currency, expressed in fey tokens or temple coins.

### Inflation

When the overall price of goods in an economy rises, that’s inflation. The key to understanding inflation is to think about how much people value goods vs. how much they value the currency used to price those goods.

If demand for goods rises much faster than supply and the currency in circulation remains steady, the amount of money people are willing to pay also rises. You get the same result if the amount of currency rises dramatically, but the supply remains steady.

Inflation also happens when trust in a currency drops. If people think a nation’s currency will soon be melted down and recoined by a conqueror, they value it less. This can become a self-reinforcing cycle, because inflation makes people lose trust in the currency and declining trust spurs further inflation.

Typically, when inflation strikes, people spend money to buy goods that hold their value, especially land, armaments, magic items, spellbooks, livestock—anything that remains in demand. People who don’t have that much money find life difficult as they struggle to keep up with costs. High inflation is destructive, but modest inflation can be helpful for trade, because it tends to discourage hoarding currency. People spend money now rather than later, so a merchant gets the sales.

### Currency Conversion

Currency itself can be a commodity. Neighboring nations might have their own coinage, yet they want to trade. To buy, a nation needs a supply of its counterpart’s currency.

Generally, the larger an economy is, the higher the demand is for its currency. Currencies experiencing strong inflation or a weak economy tend to be in low demand.

Because most fantasy currencies are gold, silver, and copper coins, they can trade at a relatively consistent rate of exchange since one nation’s currency can always be reminted into another. The rate of exchange reflects the relative weight and purity of each realm’s coins.

### Stores of Value

Even in an economy where currency is common, durable goods are kept as a store of value. This protects merchants and nobles against inflation or turmoil that could make a currency worthless.

#### Gems

Gemstones are great at storing value. They’re rare, they’re durable, they’re pretty to look at, and each one is unique. They’re also compact, so you can carry a lot of wealth in a small package.

The disadvantage of gems is that they are the thousand dollar bill of fantasy. No one pays for bread and milk with an emerald. It can be hard to find a buyer for gems, which in turn can depress their price if you need to sell them quickly. If a PC needs to liquidate a gem immediately, the GM might reasonably give only half as much as the gem’s appraised value, although that’s a trick to use sparingly.

#### Gold and Silver

Precious metals such as gold and silver have many advantages as currency. Their rarity and cost to mine discourages inflation, but they’re still obtainable. Gold doesn’t tarnish or corrode, making it an excellent long term marker of wealth. Silver, as a readily available metal, is more common for day-to-day transactions, while gold is reserved for durable goods and large scale trade.

Both metals are easily measurable by weight, which makes it easy to mint them into different denominations.

#### Magic Items

Magic items, especially permanent ones, are an excellent store of value in the same way as art or real estate. The time and expertise required to create them ensures they are relatively rare, and they offer the owner not only a reliable store of value, but also a tool for gaining additional income through adventure or war.

Like gems and jewelry, the high value of these items can make them difficult to sell, requiring brokers or middlemen. However, not only the wealthy buy them. Adventurers and their ilk often pay hefty sums for martial magic items, enchanted grimoires, and sources of arcane power.

#### Banking

Currency allows for the creation of banks which protect the money deposited and use it to offer loans that pay interest to depositors and support the banker’s operations.

The earliest forms of banking were arrangements where one farmer provided seed for crop planting to others working on new land, in exchange for a share of the harvest. This replenished the original seed stock and brought a profit to the lender.

Fantasy banks can be more anachronistic. They use gold currency for capital investments rather than for buying commodities. Established banks can also issue bank notes which can be traded instead of currency, although this might create tension with local government. However, a bank won’t honor a stolen bank note that was reported. Theft is less attractive in this case, and, bank scrip is easier to carry or store in secret than a chest of coins.

#### Borrowing

Because fantasy banks operate in a world of considerable risk (including dragons, undead, and evil high priests), loans require substantial collateral. Anyone who takes a loan to start a business must offer the business itself or other valuable property as collateral, such as magic items, jewelry, land deeds, ship titles, and deeds to feudal rights.

Stability also matters in large-scale lending. For loans to flourish, the political authorities must enforce private contracts. Bankers and merchants hate uncertainty, asset seizures, and corrupt authorities, because it disrupts their carefully managed risk.

Small loans are handled by smaller, sometimes sketchy operators who use hired thugs to collect payments or dues by force. These aren’t banks, though they serve a similar role for those without collateral or government protection.

### Financial Crimes

Financial crimes rarely involve violence. Instead, they harm someone’s property or misuse finances. Most fantasy societies don’t have investigative police or agencies that pursue strictly financial crimes. Instead, individuals must find the criminal or hire an agent to do it. Only then can they petition a magistrate or other officer for redress.

Smaller matters are settled following local custom— shopkeepers, cheats, and gamblers are often beaten in public, shamed, or even exiled if their offense is against an entire community.

#### Bribery

When an official receives something of value for breaking or bending the rules, that’s corrupt in some societies. In other places, it’s just how they do business.

Bribery and corruption can be difficult to prove. Among financial crimes, it’s one of the most common and least punished. Vigilante action or dueling are sometimes the appropriate redress.

#### Counterfeiting

When someone creates currency outside the sanction of the local authority, that’s counterfeiting. In a world of magic, avenues for counterfeiting go far beyond the craftsmanship of forgery.

Illusion is the first resort of scoundrels, but also the most likely to be found out. Wise merchants touch suspicious coins with cold iron to avoid being passed leaves instead of silver. Meanwhile, transmuters and conjurers can create permanent currency at will. The only thing stopping them in most cases is that they can find bigger payout for their skills on a larger scale. However, counterfeiting is a near- perfect crime for spellcasters of a certain persuasion.

However it’s done, counterfeiting is always a serious crime. Counterfeiting undermines confidence in the issuing authorities and can wreck an economy if it becomes rampant. It’s paired with charges of theft, since counterfeit money is almost always traded away for items of real value.

#### Fraud

Fraud covers a wide swath of financial crimes, but it essentially amounts to lying about the terms of some exchange. If you say you’ll do something and you don’t, congratulations, that’s fraud.

Defrauding a government or other powerful institution can get a fraudster clapped in irons (or worse). Like many financial crimes, defrauding someone of lesser status within a society often goes unpunished. The injured might seek good-hearted individuals to convince an authority to get justice.

#### Tax Evasion

Failing to pay taxes levied by the governing authority could potentially land someone in serious trouble. A lot depends on the specific tax system in place and who’s enforcing it. One of the more common consequences for tax evasion is simply that the authorities would forcibly seize property from the debtor or force them into working off their debt. In some cases, debtors would be imprisoned, but most regents understood that prisoners are less likely to make good on their obligations than if they were free.

#### Theft

While considered a crime in nearly every society, how it is punished can vary wildly. In cases of robbery, the victim may be given leave to apprehend the thief, reclaim their property, and possibly even kill the thief. In cases where the property goes missing, the victim can appeal to a magistrate to try and return the stolen goods.

It’s unlikely any public official would investigate a property crime on behalf of a citizen unless those services are paid for. This kind of investigative work would have to be done by the victim, their family, or hired agents.

## Trade Goods

Goods that are routinely traded by merchants and empires are lumped together as trade goods. They have four key qualities. A trade good doesn’t need to fully satisfy all four, but most trade goods do. The best trade goods have these qualities:

1. Valuable enough to cover the cost of transport.
2. Durable enough to survive transport.
3. In sufficient demand for widespread trade.
4. In sufficient supply to be reliably acquirable.

### List of Trade Goods

These trade goods generally meet these four criteria. This section contains a list of raw materials, refined goods, finished goods, and magical goods that are suitable for trade at scale. Some categories include fantasy goods such as mithral and obsidian that seldom appear in large quantities, but attract attention and money when they do. Each type of good includes the following details:

***Description.*** How the good is traded and the role it plays in a “standard” fantasy economy. Different campaign settings might use different goods. Feel free to change the names (for instance, your setting might use yak hair instead of wool for warm clothes), but consider keeping values approximately the same for ease of use.

***Unit of Measure.*** This is the unit of measure commonly used when the good is traded from one merchant to another. This also approximates how many of these units are in a ton of this trade good if tons aren’t the normal unit of measure.

***Base Price.*** The average price a buyer might pay. Price can change sharply depending on supply, demand, and desperation, but unless there’s an extraordinary market condition, let players have a good idea about price.

### Buying and Selling Trade Goods for PCs

PCs who buy and sell trade goods don’t always pay the base price, especially if they are buying from someone other than the producer of the goods. When this happens, they should pay a market price based on the market where they are trading.

Merchants are always looking to buy low and sell high. Ultimately, merchants try to take advantage of conditions with an eye to then taking advantage of future conditions.

#### Market Prices

To find the local market price for a good, multiply the base price by the local market multiplier. All market modifiers start at a value of 1 (100%).

Market tables then add or subtract from this value. The market modifier can never be lower than 0.1 (10%), but there’s no maximum value.

Three essential factors determine a market multiplier:

- **Demand.** How much of this good do the locals want to purchase?
- **Supply.** How much of this good is available in the local markets?
- **Seller.** Is this being sold by the original producer, a merchant, or a shopkeeper?

You can roll on the **Local Market Supply** and **Local Market Demand** tables, or set the value based on campaign details. The **Seller Type** table modifies the price based on who’s selling to a PC. The **Buyer Type** table modifies the price based on who a PC is selling to.

**Local Market Supply**

| 2d6 | Local Supply | Market Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Miniscule | +1 |
| 3–5 | Modest | +0.3 |
| 6–8 | Good | +0 |
| 9–11 | Abundant | −0.3 |
| 12 | Flooded | −1 |

**Local Market Demand**

| 2d6 | Local Demand | Market Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | Miniscule | −1 |
| 3–5 | Modest | −0.4 |
| 6–8 | Good | +0 |
| 9–11 | High | +0.5 |
| 12 | Craze | +2 |

**Seller Type**

| d6 | Type of Seller | Market Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Producer | +0 |
| 3–4 | Merchant or Trader | +0.5 |
| 5–6 | Shopkeeper | +1 |

**Buyer Type**

| d6 | Type of Buyer | Market Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| 1–2 | Producer | NA |
| 3–4 | Merchant or Trader | +0 |
| 5–6 | Shopkeeper | +0.5 |

Now that you have your total market modifier, multiply that by the base price of the goods to get a price for a given market transaction. For example, Syreena the Slayer wants to buy a bale of fine silks while visiting the Spider Forest. The base price for a bale of silk is 30 gp. The GM determines the local demand for silk is good (+0), the supply is abundant (−0.3), and Syreena is buying from a local producer (+0). The final market modifier is 0.7 (1 − 0.3). The GM multiplies base price (30 gp) by the market multiplier (0.7) for a purchase price of 21 gp.

After this buy, Syreena transports her bale of silk by wagon to Weaver’s Dale and sells it to a dress maker. The GM determines that the local demand for silk is high (+0.5), the local supply is good (+0), and Syreena is selling to another merchant (+0). The final market modifier (1.5) times the base price (30 gp) means the bale sells for 45 gp.

Syreena made 24 gp in profit on the venture.

## Merchant Organizations

Merchants organize themselves in many ways, from mutual aid to guild-enforced fees to formal voting groups with strict criteria for membership. Here are a few common kinds of merchant organizations.

### Guilds

A merchant guild can be anything from an informal association of marketplace stall vendors to a massive organization with its own military force and a fleet of ships. Regardless of size, the goal of a merchant guild is to help create conditions for profitable trade. The most common objectives are the physical security of traders and routes, and the desire to monopolize trade to maximize profit.

Merchant guilds are typically oligarchical, where merchant families with the greatest wealth exercise the greatest control. These families finance the guild’s treasury and armed forces. The more dues a member pays, the more power they wield in guild matters.

Guilds use their forces to guard against robbery, piracy, invading forces, rival merchants, and sometimes the local government. That last situation is rare, as most merchant guilds have a symbiotic relationship with the local authorities. It’s common for the government to officially sanction a guild’s local monopoly in return for tax payments collected by the guild.

While always interested in maximizing profit, merchant guilds often set codes of practice to avoid angering customers. Many guilds set standards for quality of goods and services and police members who commit fraud or otherwise damage the guild’s reputation.

### Collectives

Collectives are largely informal organizations without a central authority. Instead, voluntary participants in a collective contribute goods or services to the enterprise. It’s more common for farmers, craftspeople, and adventurers to form a collective than for merchants to do so.

However, merchants do sometimes form collectives to share security or maintenance responsibilities, such as running a market or training apprentices. Some larger guilds also operate in a more collective fashion, where local merchants in a city have local autonomy, but do business with members of the same guild from other cities. Collectives tend to make decisions in a democratic manner, with each member having an equal say in group decisions.

### Partnerships

Partnerships arise when a single individual or family doesn’t have all the needed resources or skills needed for an enterprise. All members of the partnership have a financial stake in the business, though not always an equal one. The dissolution of a partnership can be contentious unless all details for dissolving it were agreed upon at the founding.

Typically, a merchant’s family members aren’t considered partners, as the business passes to a merchant’s heirs upon their death.

### Companies

The idea of a company, in the modern sense, isn’t something found in a fantasy world, but aspects of it are visible. Hand crafted products such as pottery, metalwork, paintings, or jewelry often had a maker’s mark indicating who made it or which firm sold it. Inns, stores, and other shops catering to lower income customers often have names, frequently represented with an “icon,” a simple image to help the illiterate find their goods.

Most businesses are owned by a single individual or family, so the reputation of the owner and the business are closely linked. If a business trades hands, it often changes significantly under the new owner.

### Government Backed

Nations and their rulers routinely engage in mercantile activities, even outside their borders. Part of being a merchant is being neither a landowner nor a member of government. However, larger merchant guilds and religious orders can grow into nations of their own. Some nations have always funded mercantile efforts—reducing risks in exchange for a lion’s share of the profits.

These government-backed efforts can even define some functions of the state. Think of Spanish galleons carrying gold, Venetian trade galleys loaded with spice and protected by the Republic, or shipments of bronze owned by an ancient Greek city-state. When a monarch wants to enrich their kingdom, the lines between a private venture and royal charter can get blurry.

Enterprises run or protected by governments have motives beyond profit-seeking. They might secure military supplies, fund new settlements, or create an outlet for young nobles to go make their name. State-owned mercantile ventures may participate in espionage, operate at a loss for political reasons, or enforce their monopoly via law.

### Public Service

While merchants can be stereotyped as greedy cutthroats, their best interests often align with keeping their home territory productive and prosperous. Merchant guilds sometimes build and maintain roads between communities, and often organize patrols on trading routes, which benefits everyone. They encourage industries and local wealth so they can buy and sell off of them. Governments often borrow money from guilds to build public works or pay for military defenses.

Merchants also often hire adventurers to clear out troublesome monster lairs or to collect treasures they have buyers for. In short, they are often an invested part of a thriving community.
