*Optional, from Northlands Worldbook.*

Raiding has been part of Northern life since the Reaving. Raids served many purposes: winning glory, training young warriors, punishing enemies, or, most often, getting badly needed resources. A raid meant securing a ship, gathering a crew, sailing (often far) to a target, attacking it, and coming home with treasure. Played out fully, a raid could be a campaign in itself, and GMs are free to run it that way. These optional rules instead let parts of the voyage, or the whole raid, happen "off-stage" in abstract, which keeps raids fast and thrilling.

> [!note] A Word About Theft and Raiding
> To a Northlander, raiding isn't thievery. Stealing is dishonorable and breaks the rules of hospitality. Raiding isn't theft, because you fight for the treasure. Even if the "battle" is sneaking up on a place, setting it on fire, and cutting down the people as they flee, it counts as a fight and an honorable way to win the rewards. For a reaver, anything goes as long as it doesn't stain their honor.

The usual goal of a raid isn't to take land or do the most damage, but to carry off the most resources or treasure without losing too many warriors or supplies. Raids are lightning-fast assaults, not sieges.

## Using the Raiding Rules

These rules break a raid into abstract phases (preparation, journey, raid, and return), each decided by dice and tables, with the results of each phase affecting the next.

***Cost.*** To join a raid, each character pays coin based on the target (see the **Raiding Targets** table). This is their share of outfitting the expedition, and in return they get a bigger share of the plunder.

***Time.*** Raiding activities are measured in weeks of at least 5 days each, with 8 hours a day spent on the current activity. Each phase says how many weeks it takes.

***Distance.*** The GM needs to know how far away the target is, but the exact distance matters little to these rules. More distant targets may lead the GM to adjust later phases for local climates, hazards, plants, and animals.

***Raiding Roll and Spoils Dice.*** The raid and its plunder work much like an attack and damage roll. The characters make a raiding roll against a DC set by the size of the target (plus any modifiers), and if they succeed, roll a spoils die to see how much plunder they take.

One spoil is worth 500 gp, whether it's a bag of grain, bars of steel, a pouch of gems, or a crate of chickens. Spoils don't replace normal treasure; they are shorthand for the wealth taken. The spoils rolled are what goes directly to the characters, assuming the NPCs have already been paid their own share.

Any phase can be replaced with actual play. For example, the preparation, journey, and return could be abstract while the raid itself is run as a full combat encounter.

## Raiding Phases

***Preparation.*** The characters choose:

- *The Target.* The place to raid.
- *Ship Upgrades.* Extra improvements for the ship.
- *Additional Raiders.* A crew of hearty warriors.
- *Special Resources.* Provisions, repair materials, and other details for the journey.
- *The Cost.* Once the total is calculated from these choices, each character pays a share.

Wise choices here can mean the difference between a successful raid and an abject failure.

***Journey.*** The characters face the hazards and obstacles of the voyage to the target. Success or failure can give bonuses or penalties in the next phase.

***Raid.*** The raiders reach their target and strike fast, before more defenders can gather. They make a raiding roll and roll the spoils die to see how well the raid went and how much they took. How it goes can make the trip home easier or harder.

***Return.*** The characters head home, hopefully loaded with spoils. Like the journey, the return has hazards, and it can be harder if the raid went badly.

***Outcome.*** When all the phases are done, the GM tallies the good and bad results of the whole voyage to find the final spoils.

## Preparation Phase

The preparation phase means recruiting warriors, upgrading a ship, and gathering supplies, but the first decision is the target.

***The Target.*** Targets range from small hamlets to forts to major cities. The danger, including armed defenders, grows with the size of the target, and so does the reward. Use the **Raiding Targets** table to choose a target and see the kinds of plunder usually found there. If the GM plans to run the raid as an encounter, the second table shows the usual number of defenders. The number of reavers needed is the same either way.

***Ship Upgrades.*** The ship, warriors, and supplies are abstracted: the characters' payment includes a suitable ship and enough warriors to attempt the chosen target. (For ships in more detail, see [[Ships and Sea Combat]] and the longships on the [[Vehicles#Longships|Vehicles]] page. Every crew member is expected both to sail the ship and to fight in the raid.)

The characters can also buy upgrades for their ship. Each gives a bonus to the raiding roll, spoils die, or another mechanic, at extra cost. Each upgrade can be used once per raid, for a bonus in one phase, and is wasted if unused. Upgrades have no effect in naval combat.

**Ship Upgrades**

| Upgrade | Bonus | Cost (gp)* |
|---|---|---|

| Hull reinforcement | Negate the monster attack event in the journey or return phase | 100 |
| Keel reinforcement | Negate the time penalty from storms in the journey or return phase | 150 |
| Larger oars | Reduce time in the journey or return phase by 1d4 days | 25 |
| Lateen sails | Reduce time in the journey or return phase by 1d4 days | 25 |
| Narrow hull | Reduce time in the journey or return phase by 1d4 days | 25 |
| Secured cargo hold | Negate the loss of spoils from the pirate event in the journey or return phase | 50 |

*Added to the price each character pays at the end of the preparation phase.

***Additional Raiders.*** The payment includes enough warriors for the chosen target, but some parties want more to make sure of victory. Characters can't empty a village of warriors to crew their longship; at best they'll persuade half its able-bodied reavers to sign on. Raiding is a risk for the community left behind too: every raider lost is one fewer warrior to defend against monsters and raids, and one fewer worker for vital tasks.

Generally, the size of the community the raid leaves from decides how many extra warriors are available. They are hired in bands of 5 to 100, each giving a bonus to the raiding roll in the raid phase at a cost added to what the characters pay (see the **Extra Warriors** table). A raid can add only one band of extra warriors.

**Extra Warriors**

| Warrior Band | Settlement Size | Raiding Roll Bonus | Cost (gp)* |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | Hamlet | +1 | 100 |
| 10 | Village | +2 | 200 |
| 25 | Small town | +3 | 500 |
| 75 | Large town | +4 | 1,500 |
| 100 | City | +5 | 2,000 |

*Added to the price each character pays at the end of the preparation phase.

***Special Resources.*** The base payment covers a decent amount of provisions, repair materials, and other supplies. The characters can also buy special resources (see the **Special Resources** table). Like ship upgrades, each gives a bonus and can be used once per raid, in one phase, and is wasted if unused.

> [!note] Who Leads the Raid?
> Are the characters leading the raid, or joining one led by an NPC? Either way, the players make the decisions and have a degree of narrative control over the NPCs involved. If they join an NPC's raid, what they pay up front is something like an investment: if the raid succeeds, they get a bigger share than the average warrior. GMs can have NPC leaders push for bigger shares, perhaps setting up some fun roleplaying confrontations.

## The Journey

In the second phase, the characters face the voyage to their target, overcoming hazards and obstacles, and success or failure can give bonuses or penalties in the next phase. They make a group roll on the **Sea/Land Journey** table for each week of travel, and the GM can add extra rolls. To bring out the epic feel of the raid, the GM should be as descriptive as possible.

***Abstracted Time and Distance.*** Instead of tracking speeds and distances, the journey takes 1 week for most destinations. The GM can add time for setting reasons or distant targets.

**Sea/Land Journey**

| d12 | Event | Additional Effects |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Storm! | Travel time increases by 1d10 days; crew impatient, −1 to spoils die |
| 2 | Monster attack | Lose some crew defeating monster; −1 to raiding roll |
| 3 | Frozen in ice | Many perish from intense cold; −2 to raiding roll |
| 4–6 | No event | — |
| 7 | Lost! | Travel time increases by 1d4 days |
| 8 | Spoiled supplies | Many warriors fall ill; −1 to raiding roll, −1 to spoils die |
| 9 | Pirates! | Lose some crew fending off the pirates; −1 to raiding roll |
| 10 | Fast travel | Reduce travel time by 1d4 days |
| 11 | Blessing of [[Njord]] | Reduce travel time by 2d4 days, crew well-fed by fish |
| 12 | [[Wotan]]'s gift | Crew inspired by dream of heroes and glory; advantage on raiding roll |

> [!note] Land Raiding
> Not every raid goes by sea. The giants' communities to the north are tempting targets for metal, rare resources, hides, and relics. Because of the North's geography, most reavers use ships, either to strike their target directly or to reach a landing site near it for an overland attack. Raiders don't have to use ships if they can march to the target, though most warriors won't want to trek hundreds of miles over frozen mountains when they could just sail.

## The Raid

In the third phase, the characters reach their target and strike before more defenders can be raised against them.

***Raiding Roll.*** The moment has come! Screaming their battle cries, the reavers leap from their longships or charge across the snowy fields toward the (hopefully) unsuspecting settlement. The players make a group raiding roll against the target's DC: a d20, modified by any bonuses or penalties gained in earlier phases. If the result meets or beats the DC, the raid succeeds and grants a bonus to the spoils die and other benefits. A result below the DC means the raiders were driven off but still grabbed some plunder on the way out, with a penalty to the spoils die and perhaps other complications. The **Raiding Roll** table shows all these results.

**Raiding Roll**

| Result | Spoils Die Adjustment | Additional Effects |
|---|---|---|
| Failed by 6 or more | −4 | Lost over half the warriors; return phase rolls have disadvantage; return phase takes an extra week |
| Failed by 1 to 5 | −2 | Heavy casualties; return phase takes an extra week |
| Equal to DC | +0 | — |
| Succeeded by 1 to 5 | +1 | Common magic item found |
| Succeeded by 6 to 9 | +2 | Important NPC taken captive or uncommon magic item found |
| Succeeded by 10 or more | +4 | Important NPC taken captive or rare magic item found |
| Critical success | Special | Add the maximum possible result of the spoils die as a bonus to the spoils die roll |

***Important NPC Captives.*** A captured important NPC can be ransomed back to their family or community. Negotiations can take time, but many jarls are glad to take such captives off the characters' hands for favors, such as renown or hidden lore, or for gold, a magic item, or another reward the GM decides.

***Spoils Die.*** Once the raiding roll is settled, the characters roll the target's spoils die, adding any adjustments from this and earlier phases. The result is the characters' own share; NPC warriors get a separate share of the total loot.

## The Return

In the fourth phase, the characters travel home, hopefully laden with spoils. Repeat the journey phase, adjusting rolls as needed. This phase can be harder if the raid went badly, and failures here can cost the characters part of their spoils.

## Outcome

When all the phases are done, the GM and players tally the good and bad results of the whole voyage to find the final spoils. The characters can exchange their spoils at once for gold or standard equipment of equal value, at 500 gp per spoil.

With GM approval, characters can instead exchange spoils for magic items. What's available depends on the size of the settlement they return to, usually the one they left from, though it needn't be. The **Spoils Exchange** table shows how many spoils buy an item of each rarity and the smallest settlement likely to have one.

What item the characters get should be discussed between players and GM. Rarity covers a wide range of value, so not every item is available just because the characters have enough spoils, and settlements might charge more than the table shows. The GM decides all availability and cost.

**Spoils Exchange**

| Number of Spoils | Magic Item Rarity | Minimum Settlement Size |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Common | Village |
| 4 | Uncommon | Small town |
| 7 | Rare | Large town |
| 10 | Very rare | City |

> [!note] Raids as Downtime Activities
> These rules can be used as a downtime activity. That works best when all the characters go on the raid together, especially since most raids take at least two weeks. GMs can shorten that by giving the expedition some of the ship upgrades or special resources that cut sailing time, which can help if some characters go raiding while others stay behind, so everyone stays on the same timeline.
