## Flyting

Feasts in the [[Northlands]] are rowdy, full of boasting, drinking, and carousing. Sometimes a jarl wants to size up a visitor and test their wits, or a quarrel needs defusing before it becomes a duel, or a murder that starts a feud lasting generations. That is what flyting is for.

A flyting is a duel of insults. Two participants take turns insulting each other in more and more creative ways until anger or embarrassment leaves one of them speechless. It can be entertainment, a way to settle a simple quarrel, or a way to calm a tense moment. It usually starts when one person challenges another publicly over something not serious enough for a duel, such as a verbal insult or a social misstep.

Facts don't matter in a flyting. Whether the insults are true counts for less than the speaker's rhyming skill and humor, and the winner is whoever best sways the audience.

***Rhythm and Rhyme.*** The challenger gives an insult and the challenged replies with one of their own. The reply must copy the rhythm of the challenger's insult, and the last words of both must rhyme. For example, if [[Loki]] is taunted with "Foul deceiver, you are a snake with an uncouth manner!" he might reply, "Pale braggart, you wield your wit like a drooping hammer."

***How Many Rounds?*** That depends on the participants, but usually 3 to 5 exchanges in all. The GM decides how many before the contest starts.

***Flyting Steps.*** When a flyting is called, follow these steps.

- ***Appoint a Judge.*** The two participants agree on a judge from among those present, usually someone neutral or unconnected to either of them. The judge gauges the audience's reaction to decide who swayed more of them. At the table, the judge is usually the other players and the GM.
- ***Participants Face Off.*** They usually stand on a long table in the longhouse, or somewhere else they can be seen and heard.
- ***Challenger Goes First.*** This is usual. If not, the two roll initiative to see who begins.
- ***Round 1: Opening Insults.*** The first round is often part boast, part statement of the grievance, and part insult.
- ***Rounds Continue.*** The rounds go on until the agreed number is reached, one participant stumbles (can't answer cleverly, repeats themselves, or fails to rhyme), or the judge declares that the audience has chosen a winner.

***Rewards.*** Often the participants' honor, or the outcome of some squabble, is at stake. It is very dishonorable to dispute the result of a flyting or seek revenge over one. At a feast, the usual prize is mead. Winning a flyting can count as a deed (see [[Reputation]]), as your GM decides.

### Easing Flyting Gameplay

Some players will find their characters are far better at spontaneous rhyming insults than they are. To help, the table below lists words and their rhymes, and players can also use kennings (below).

When using the table, the challenger must end their insult with a word from one of the rows, so the other participant can simply pick a rhyming word from the same row. Both participants must agree whether to use the table or go freestyle.

## Kennings

A kenning is a poetic play on words that replaces a concept, usually a single word, with more figurative language. A popular kenning for the sea, for example, was "whale's road." Kennings are tied to skaldic poems and draw on cultural and mythic knowledge, and they add flavor and a strong Northern feel to riddles, songs, and prophecies.

***Using Kennings in Flyting.*** Kennings make wonderful poetic weapons in a flyting duel.

***Using Kennings in Prophecies.*** Kennings suit [[Prophecies and Omens|prophecies]] well, because the wordplay in their metaphors allows many readings. In a prophecy, "Night's Ship" could mean the moon, a shadowy vessel, or perhaps a dream.

***How to Create Kennings.*** The usual way is to combine a noun and a verb, or two nouns, in either order, into a two-word phrase used in place of a more obvious or literal word. Sometimes three or more words are needed to make the meaning work. Two more examples appear in the sample prophecies on the [[Prophecies and Omens]] page: "wound-brand" for a sword and "dew of slaughter" for blood.
