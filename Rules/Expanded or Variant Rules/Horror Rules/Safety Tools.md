Running a horror campaign is similar in many ways to running any other campaign, but the thematic focus requires both the players and the GM to be familiar with the expectations of the horror genre and the situations that might arise at the table. Everyone benefits by learning about the different horror subgenres and their associated elements and by having a candid discussion about which topics and subgenres are acceptable to explore and which are off-limits. In particular, it’s a good idea to make sure that everyone understands both what kind of game will be played and also how to handle any issues with the content matter. Session zero is a perfect time to have these discussions, and that can help a GM feel all the more confident and prepared once the campaign kicks into gear.

## Session Zero

Holding a session zero is good practice for any new campaign, but it’s particularly important for horror, which forces PCs into potentially difficult, unsettling, and upsetting situations that evoke feelings of discomfort and even fear. Though this is part of the “fun” in a horror game for many players, anyone can have certain topics or elements they wish to avoid playing out—or even avoid talking about. And there’s nothing wrong with that.

This could be as straightforward as a player with a fear of spiders or as complex as a deep-seated trauma that a player would rather avoid dredging up during a game meant to be fun. Holding a session zero allows a group to implement safety tools to help avoid such moments and to discuss how to handle them if they do arise. Plus, a session zero provides the GM the perfect platform to establish expectations for player behavior at the table. For more details on how to run a session zero, see the *Game Master’s Guide.*

### Safety Tools

Safety tools are a set of rules and practices meant to ensure that everyone continues having fun. It provides a framework to empower anyone at the table to interrupt the game without recrimination or ridicule, if an unexpected and unwanted topic arises. These rules might include anonymous questionnaires handed out by the GM, candid one-on-one conversations or group discussions, and other safety tools: anything needed and agreed upon based on the comfort level of the gaming group. For more details on safety tools, see the *Player’s Guide*.

### Emotional Bleed

The concept of emotional bleed refers to when a PC’s emotions cross over to the player, or vice-versa. Roleplaying games allow us to step into someone else’s shoes and craft a story for them, and while some folks can easily maintain the division between their own feelings and their PC’s, other folks might experience bleed more regularly—and intense roleplaying or huge plot moments can make it more challenging to keep them separate.

Emotional bleed is neither inherently good nor bad. However, some players might need extra time to process the unexpected emotions it can elicit. The bleed also means a player’s emotions can unconsciously influence their PC’s actions. If a player had a particularly bad day, for example, their PC might be either more brusque or more prone to outbursts than usual. Allowing space to process this bleed, particularly in horror, helps keep everyone at the table comfortable and enjoying the game.

### Table Rules

Many tables have a handful of unofficial rules for play, such as taking a break at the midpoint of a session or discouraging phone use, and establishing the same sort of guidelines for behavior at the table during a horror campaign can help everyone have their best experience. These rules often contribute to an immersive atmosphere, but they can also support respectful table behavior in general. For example, if two PCs are engaged in a moment of roleplaying, then any crosstalk or other distracting behavior from the other players might pull the roleplayers out of the moment—and could even come across as disrespectful. Especially in horror, the GM might be trying to achieve a certain effect, so additional guidelines around table etiquette might be required.
