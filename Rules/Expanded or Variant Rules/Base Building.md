A base is a location owned and operated by the PCs. It can be the party’s primary residence or one of many properties they routinely visit to rest and regroup. It’s the location they most often return to between adventures and a place ideally suited for performing downtime activities, teambuilding, personal growth, and socializing.

PCs can expand, build, and personalize a base to best suit the needs of the group. They can also hire personnel, house-friendly NPCs or creatures, and otherwise populate their base to create a thriving headquarters. This section presents the rules for acquiring and upgrading a base and the guidelines for developing story prompts and worldbuilding mysteries connected to it.

## Acquiring a Base

Bases are primarily acquired through one of two ways: purchased by the PCs or gifted as a quest reward. There is no level requirement PCs must meet before acquiring a base, and acquisition can occur whenever it makes sense from a narrative or financial perspective.

***Purchasing a Base.*** Buying a base is no small financial investment. However, even low-level PCs can manage to earn enough treasure to purchase a “starter base.” These are typically properties that are small, neglected, and possibly in serious disrepair—which dramatically reduces their cost.

***Quest Reward.*** A GM can gift the PCs a starter base as a reward for a quest. Perhaps the party pleased the town’s mayor, who gifts them an abandoned townhouse on the outskirts of the region. Perhaps the party cleared bandits from an abandoned keep, and the king wishes for them to take ownership, so the villains won’t return. Or perhaps a corrupt government official has their schemes revealed by the party’s meddling, and the rest of the city council gifts the official’s stripped holdings to them in gratitude.

### Starter Base

When the PCs first acquire a starter base, it’s typically a pre-existing structure in the city, town, or region where they frequently return to between quests. Inhabitable structures are humble and typically in need of improvement or repair, and examples might include overgrown estates, crumbling keeps, and half-burned inns. Alternatively, a GM can roll or choose an option on the **Starter Base** table to determine the nature of the base. When choosing the starter base, the GM should consider how the PCs might eventually upgrade and expand on the structure, particularly when choosing a location with space restrictions, such as a townhouse in the center of a crowded city district.

The suggested costs in the **Starter Base** table also assume it includes one of the facilities listed in the Existing Facility column and detailed in the **Facility Descriptions** section.

> [!note] Customizing Starter Bases
> As GM, you know the needs of your game and PCs the best. Feel free to mix and match the elements found in the **Starter Base** table or create your own as you see fit. For instance, you could create a crumbling keep with a library or design something altogether new.
>

#### Starter Base Size and Dimensions

Regardless of a starter base’s general type and location, it includes a certain amount of usable space, which must be allocated in such a way as to accommodate a central common area, basic utilities like a rudimentary kitchen and privies, and private quarters for each of the PCs. A base’s overall size and potential space can vary widely, but the size of the building’s current usable space is calculated as follows, in 5-foot squares:

**Usable space = 16 squares × the number of PCs** Once a base’s usable space is determined, the players should work to design the overall base layout together. For example, a party of four PCs would have a base with a floor plan composed of 64 usable squares. These could then be arranged so that each PC has a cramped bedroom of 4 squares each, leaving 48 squares to be divided among the common spaces. Or alternatively, the PCs might have more expansive personal quarters, leaving smaller common spaces. Squares can be placed spread out over multiple buildings, can be stacked in several stories, or can be part of a sprawling one-story home. Once usable space is allocated, then determine the location of any existing facilities, which don’t subtract from the initial usable space (see **Facility Descriptions** for the amount of space taken up by each).

Though the initial usable space for the starter base is limited, the base itself might have land or other currently unusable or inaccessible areas that can be renovated or expanded by the PCs at a later date. For example, if the PCs acquired a vacant estate as their starter base, there might be rooms, wings, or outbuildings that are partially collapsed or otherwise useless until repairs are made, giving the PCs space to expand and grow their base as the campaign progresses.

When creating a starter base, a GM should keep space for expansion in mind, as well as any mysteries that might be uncovered during future renovations (see **Mysteries**).

### Expanding Usable Space

If the existing base doesn’t have available unused space to install a given facility, the PCs can construct additional space at a cost of 100 gp per 5-foot square (GM’s discretion). The construction of the additional space takes 2 days per square.

### Upgrading a Base

Once the PCs have acquired a base, they can make additions or alterations to better fit their lifestyles and can hire staff to help with operations and upkeep. Many of these upgrades grant benefits to the downtime activities associated with that particular upgrade. See the *Game Master’s Guide and* *Player’s Guide* for the rules for downtime activities.

There are two types of upgrades: facilities and personnel.

## Facilities

Facilities are rooms or areas within a base that give the PCs specific resources or benefits. Starter bases typically come with at least one pre-constructed facility. PCs can add additional facilities to their base as long as the requirements are met. Each facility has a cost, minimum space requirement, and time required to complete the facility. Unless otherwise stated, more than one of the same facility type can be installed in a base.

### Elements of a Facility

This section details the components found in each facility description.

#### Cost

The cost of installing a facility includes the renovation of the space itself but also the basic materials and supplies needed to operate the facility for the first month. For example, a tavern would come with mugs, plates, and the like, plus common wine and ale and the ingredients for cooking common meals.

#### Space Requirement

The space requirement listed is the smallest area that the facility can be installed in. It can also be installed in a larger space at no additional cost, though this comes with no added benefits. The space requirement assumes that the facility is being installed in an existing unused space within the base, and the associated cost includes renovating that space. “Unused space” can be any room or other suitable area that does not currently have a facility within it. For example, a PC might install a studio in their bedroom, or they might replace the simple kitchen with a chef’s kitchen. The space needed for a facility does not need to be enclosed or otherwise divided from other facilities. For example, multiple studios can be installed in a single large room with ample space, giving the area an open, collaborative feel.

#### Time

Each facility has a listed time. This is the number of days of work necessary to complete the construction of the facility. These days are typically consecutive, but there are no penalties for interrupting the construction and continuing at a later time. This assumes hired builders, artisans, and similar specialists are doing the work, so the PCs are free to come and go from the base as they wish without disrupting ongoing construction.

#### Personnel

Each facility lists the types of personnel that can work in it. In most cases, the PCs themselves can operate a facility, but they might wish to hire personnel to operate or maintain it in their absence. In many cases, personnel also provide added benefit to the facility. An individual can work at multiple facilities as time and logistics allow. For example, the same performance troupe can play at a party in a ballroom and also put on shows in a theater as long as those events do not overlap. More information can be found in the **Personnel** section of this chapter.

#### Downtime Activities

If a facility interacts with or grants an additional benefit when certain downtime activities are performed within its confines, those activities are listed in each entry.

### Replacing Facilities

At any point, the PCs can destroy an existing facility to reclaim the space it occupies or to replace it with a new facility. Once a facility is destroyed, its benefits are unusable, and all of its assets are lost, including the gold spent to construct it. Any hired personnel that list the previous facility as a prerequisite are also lost unless the new facility or another existing facility satisfies their prerequisite.

### Creating Custom Facilities

The GM or a player might wish to create facilities that aren’t listed here to better suit the campaign and the PCs’ needs. The following guidance can help determine the aspects of the facility, including cost, space requirements, potential personnel, possible maintenance costs, and any downtime or other benefits conveyed by the facility.

When creating a custom facility, first come up with the concept. What is the purpose of this facility? In some cases, it might be easiest to take an existing facility and modify it. However, if this doesn’t make sense for the new purpose, consider what might be contained within the facility itself, including any furniture, equipment, or tools needed to allow the facility to function. Once that’s been determined, consider the following.

***Cost.*** Pricing a facility requires taking several variables into account. Generally, the more equipment, space, and time required to build it, the less it will cost to simply install.

Typically, the price of a facility falls between 500 gp and 1,200 gp.

***Space Required.*** The space required for a facility depends on the activities and equipment necessary for it to function. For example, a tailor’s workshop that holds a workbench and shelves of fabric might take up far less space than a sprawling ballroom meant for large gatherings.

***Time.*** Once you know what items and equipment the facility requires, as well as the amount of space it takes up, you can determine the amount of time it will take to install. Smaller or simpler facilities take less time to build than larger or more elaborate ones, and specialized facilities, such as reinforced or magically protected ones, take much longer. Most facilities take between 14 and 30 days to install.

***Personnel.*** To determine what, if any, personnel would potentially be associated with the facility, consider the following questions. Does the facility require someone to run it, like a shop or tavern? Does it require a highly skilled individual to operate it, or can it be run by someone with minimal training? Check the existing list of personnel to see if it makes sense for any of those types to be associated with the facility or create your own personnel (see **Personnel**).

***Downtime Activities.*** Consider what downtime activities might be associated with the facility, if any. Is it a place for carousing and socializing? Or is it a space for crafting items of a specific type? Are there any advantages for conducting the downtime activity in the facility? For example, a PC crafting an item in a fully stocked studio has access to materials and tools they likely won’t have on the road, so it is typically quicker and easier for them to craft items in such a space. Similarly, PCs with a ballroom might gain additional carousing benefits by impressing their guests with such a beautiful, opulent space. If there don’t seem to be any suitable downtime activities, don’t worry because they’re not required. For more on downtime activities, see **Between Adventures** in **Chapter 6** of the *Player’s Guide*.

***Special Benefits.*** Some facilities, such as the chapel and the infirmary, convey special benefits to PCs who spend time within them. These are rare and often found only in those facilities that have no other mechanical or downtime benefit.

*A laboratory for crafting alchemical concoctions and magical* *consumables.*

**Cost:** 1,000 gp

**Space Requirement:** 9 squares

**Time:** 14 days

**Personnel:** Alchemist

**Downtime Activities:** Crafting, Potion Brewing, Training An alchemical laboratory functions similarly to a standard crafting workshop. However, due to the specialty materials required for the crafting of alchemical substances and other concoctions, and the delicate nature of the work done within, the space must be specially constructed.

***Alchemist Tools.*** Creatures are considered to have alchemist tools while in the laboratory. PCs proficient with alchemist tools have advantage on ability checks made with them while within this facility.

***Crafting.*** A PC can use the crafting downtime activity (see *Player’s Guide*) to craft alchemical concoctions in the laboratory. Crafting here is more efficient than at other locations. For each workday spent crafting, a PC or alchemist (see **Personnel**) can craft one item (or multiple items) worth a total of 15 gp. A PC must still cover the costs of required materials and follow other crafting requirements.

#### Ballroom

*An elegant room for decadent soirees.*

**Cost:** 2,000 gp

**Space Requirement:** 50 squares

**Time:** 30 days

**Personnel:** Performer, Servant

**Downtime Activities:** Carousing (Upper Class) A ballroom is a vast room with hardwood or marble floors, high ceilings, and beautiful ornamentation. Used for hosting parties, balls, and other large social events, a ballroom typically features a central dance floor, tables for drinks and other refreshments, and an area for musicians, though it can be rearranged to suit the specific needs of a particular event.

***Carousing.*** A PC can spend 1 week of downtime performing the carousing activity (see *Player’s Guide*) with the upper class by hosting events in the ballroom. You don’t need to pay the typical 250 gp cost, and you have advantage on the CHA (Persuasion) check made to determine the carousing resolution result when you spend downtime in this way.

***Well Appointed.*** If a retained performer or servant (see **Personnel**) is assigned to the ballroom when a PC makes the week’s carousing resolution check, the PC can treat any d20 roll of 9 or less as if they had rolled a 10 on the die.

#### Barracks

*For the long-term housing of soldiers.*

**Cost:** 1,200 gp (additional monthly costs)

**Space Requirement:** 16 squares

**Time:** 20 days

**Personnel:** Guard Captain, Soldier

**Downtime Activities:** None A barracks is a building, or rooms, dedicated to long-term housing for soldiers or other martial personnel. It holds three bunked cots, enough space for up to six soldiers.

***High Morale.*** Soldiers housed in the barracks have advantage on saves against becoming charmed or frightened.

***Monthly Costs.*** A barracks offers comfortable lodging, food, and other amenities that appeal to skilled hirelings. For this reason, soldiers are willing to work for half their typical rate when housed in the barracks. This discount is already factored into the monthly rates listed in the soldier entry of the **Personnel** section of this chapter. You must pay each retained soldier their listed monthly rate to keep them housed in the barracks, or they abandon both your cause and your base. If your barracks are empty, it doesn’t incur any monthly costs.

#### Bathhouse

*A peaceful space for relaxation and recovery.*

**Cost:** 1,200 gp

**Space Requirement:** 16 squares

**Time:** 30 days

**Personnel:** Servant

**Downtime Activities:** None A bathhouse is a room or collection of rooms that hold baths and other spa-like amenities, such as massage tables, steam rooms, and saunas. A standard bathhouse contains one 20-foot-by-20-foot pool or four individual tubs. The bathhouse requires little maintenance, but PCs can hire servants to provide massages or other treatments, serve drinks and food, or act as general attendants to any bathhouse guests.

***Relaxed and Recharged.*** A creature that spends 8 hours (can be done over the course of a long rest) relaxing in a bathhouse is cured of all levels of exhaustion. Alternatively, if a creature is suffering from a multi-day effect caused by a disease, curse, spell, or something similar, such as the penalties caused by the *raise dead spell* *(see Player’s Guide)*, spending 8 hours in the bathhouse reduces the effect as if 2 days have passed (instead of 1).

***Well Appointed.*** If a PC spends at least 1 hour in the bathhouse receiving a massage or other treatment from a retained servant (see **Personnel**), they gain temporary hit points equal to twice their PB. These temporary hit points last until expended or until the PC finishes their next long rest.

#### Brewery

*An area for brewing ale and beer.*

**Cost:** 1,200 gp

**Space Requirement:** 16 squares

**Time:** 30 days

**Personnel:** Brewer

**Downtime Activities:** Crafting A brewery is a large space filled with mills, furnaces, wooden barrels, glass jugs, and other heavy-duty equipment for the crafting of ale and beer.

***Crafting.*** A PC proficient with provisioner tools can use the crafting downtime activity (see *Player’s Guide*) to produce barrels of ale or beer here. Due to the time needed to complete the fermenting process, crafting in a brewery requires a minimum time requirement of at least 2 weeks of downtime and other creatures can’t assist to speed up the process. A brewer (see **Personnel**) can also craft barrels for the PCs. Either way, a PC must cover the costs of required materials, in addition to meeting other crafting requirements.

***Rate of Production.*** Due to the large volume of goods produced by a brewery, the required crafting time, amounts produced, and costs are different from the rules governing standard crafting. See the **Batch Brewing** table for these requirements. Note that a brewer can craft multiple barrels at once, making them more efficient than PCs.

#### Chapel

*A quiet space for worship.*

**Cost:** 1,200 gp

**Space Requirement:** 16 squares

**Time:** 30 days

**Personnel:** Chaplain

**Downtime Activities:** None A chapel is a place of worship. It can be tailored to a specific deity or group of deities or to a particular faith, or it can be agnostic, accommodating folks of all paths and faiths in finding comfort and a space to meditate or pray. It might be made of stone, with soaring ceilings, long pews, and stained glass, or it might be a simple building with rugs and cushions on the floor and candles or incense for offerings.

***Inner Peace.*** A PC who spends at least 1 hour a day for 7 consecutive days praying or meditating in the chapel gains advantage on WIS saves for the next 7 days. In addition, a cleric that uses their Divine Intervention feature within the chapel can add double their PB to determine the roll result.

**Cost:** 5,000 gp

**Space Requirement:** 1 square

**Time:** 30 days

**Personnel:** None

**Downtime Activities:** None An escape tunnel is a passage that leads either from one area of the base to another or from the base to a point offsite. #### Garden

*A cultivated outdoor space for growing flowers, herbs, or vegetables.*

**Cost:** 800 gp

**Space Requirement:** 16 squares

**Time:** 15 days

**Personnel:** Herbalist

**Downtime Activities:** Plant Care A garden is a plot of land that is tilled and tended to grow plants, and it comes with two sets of herbalist tools. It can be an open space for growing grains or vegetables, an orchard thick with fruit or nut trees, or a maze of winding pathways lined with sculpted topiaries and fragrant blossoms. During construction, the PCs should decide the layout and what plants it will contain. There is no benefit for building a garden bigger than the required space, but more than one garden can be installed in the base.

***Garden of Plenty.*** The type of garden dictates the type of harvest, as indicated by the **Garden Harvest** table. Each 5-foot square takes 1 hour to harvest. The harvest can be sold at market, used as materials for crafting, or used to support the base’s personnel.

***Plant Care.*** A garden requires a PC or herbalist (see **Personnel**) to tend to it at least 3 days a week, to water, weed, trim, and otherwise cultivate the plants. If left untended, it can become overgrown, diseased, or riddled with pests, or fruits might rot on the vine. A neglected garden can be brought back to full functionality for half the associated cost and time.

#### Gearworks

*A workshop for building and repairing mechanical contraptions.*

**Cost:** 600 gp

**Space Requirement:** 16 squares

**Time:** 10 days

**Personnel:** Tinker

**Downtime Activities:** Crafting, Training A gearworks is a space specifically fitted for working on mechanical contraptions, clockwork devices, and vehicles. It comes with items like a workbench, hand tools, and basic materials.

***Crafting.*** A PC can use the crafting downtime activity (see *Player’s Guide*) to craft or repair mechanical contraptions or other items made with tinker tools. Crafting in the gearworks is more efficient than other locations. For each workday spent crafting, a PC or tinker (see **Personnel**) can craft one item (or multiple items) worth a total of 15 gp. A PC must still cover the costs of required materials and follow other crafting requirements.

***Tinker Tools.*** Creatures are considered to have tinker tools while in the gearworks. PCs proficient with tinker tools have advantage on ability checks made with them while within this facility.

***Training.*** A PC performing the training downtime activity to gain proficiency with tinker tools can double their PB when determining the training result for the week. The PC must have daily access to this facility and a skilled and willing instructor to gain this benefit.

***Vehicle Repairs.*** A PC proficient with a vehicle type can more efficiently repair vehicles of that type while in the gearworks. A tinker (see **Personnel**) gains these same benefits while repairing vehicles of any type. When a proficient creature performs a patch job in this facility, the vehicle gains twice the number of temporary hit points. When a proficient creature replaces parts in this facility, the vehicle regains twice the number of hit points per hour spent repairing it. PCs must still provide the required repair cost and abide by other vehicle repair restrictions specified in the *Player’s Guide.*

#### Guest Quarters

*Extra rooms in case company comes to call.*

**Cost:** 1,200 gp

**Space Requirement:** 12 squares

**Time:** 20 days

**Personnel:** Servant

**Downtime Activities:** None Guest quarters are additional bedrooms set aside for the likes of visiting dignitaries, family members of the PCs, or even other adventuring parties that are staying on a temporary basis. Each space includes a full-sized bed that can sleep two Medium Humanoids as well as other fine bedroom furniture. They might also include a sitting area, desk, bookshelves, or other furnishings to make the space feel like a home away from home.

***Grateful Guests.*** If a creature stays in the guest quarters for at least a night, the PCs gain advantage on CHA checks to interact with that creature for as long as they continue to lodge in the guest quarters.

***Well Appointed.*** If a retained servant (see **Personnel**) is assigned to the guest quarters for at least 1 week while an NPC is staying in the facility, that NPC’s attitude toward the PCs automatically increases by one step (see attitude in either *Game Master’s Guide or Player’s Guide*) at the end of that week. If the visiting NPC already has the maximum positive attitude toward the PCs, the GM can rule that this personal attention earns the PCs a favor (as per the carousing activity) from the NPC or else increases the party’s status with the NPC’s associated faction (as per the status rules in *Game Master’s Guide*).

## Personnel

Unlike hirelings, personnel are the collected individuals hired on a long-term basis to staff or assist in the operation of facilities within the PCs’ base. They can be hired at any time as long as they are paid and the requisite facilities are present. Unless otherwise stated, more than one individual of the same personnel type can be retained at the same time.

Individuals willing to work are readily found in most mid-to-large settlements, but a GM might determine that the PCs must first make a contact or fulfill some other specific obligation before they can hire a specialized type of personnel.

### Using Personnel

Unless specified otherwise, all types of personnel follow certain general rules.

***Assignment.*** At the start of each week, you can assign which facility each retained member will work in for that week. If you don’t change an individual’s assignment, they remain assigned to their previously assigned facility. As long as a member is assigned to a facility, you can safely assume they are available whenever you need them to conduct a specific activity within that facility—so you don’t need to track or plan hour-by-hour activity. However, in situations where counting hours per day or days per week matters, personnel work at the same pace as a PC would, using downtime rules. You can’t force anyone to work either more than 8 hours a day or more than 5 days a week.

***Crafting Costs.*** Many types of personnel can craft items requested by the PCs. Whenever they create, modify, or repair something, the PCs must still pay the required monetary costs. This rule must always be followed regardless of item type, circumstances, or whether the item is magical or nonmagical. The personnel never pay for material costs or any other required gold. In almost all cases, the required costs to make an item can be found in the associated downtime activity rules, like the crafting activity description in the Player’s Guide.

***Statistics.*** While personnel can be portrayed with full personalities and histories for roleplaying purposes, they are noncombatants who don’t participate in fights, encounters, or similar scenarios that might put them in harm’s way. Personnel also function differently from PCs in the way they perform tasks: they don’t make checks or utilize statistics as standard creatures do. For this reason, they are not assigned stat blocks, and it isn’t recommended that a GM assigns them stat blocks unless they have a very good reason to do so. The exceptions to this rule are the few types designed to participate in combat or similar encounter scenarios, like the soldier. These unique roles reference specific stat blocks within their descriptions.

### Elements of Personnel

This section explains the components found within the description for each type of personnel.

**Prerequisite**

Personnel typically require the PCs to have a certain facility type within the base before they can be hired. They frequently grant benefits only when they have access to their prerequisite facility. Though some, like servants, are beneficial to have in a variety of facilities.

**Cost**

The cost listed in the description for each personnel type is the gold required to retain that individual’s service for 1 month (4 weeks). This is the minimum amount of time an individual must be paid to be considered personnel and to gain the specific benefits of facilities within the base. The listed cost is commensurate with what it would be to hire them off the street. However, PCs might be able to hire or retain specific individuals or certain types of personnel for a lower rate (GM’s discretion).

**Special Abilities** Some types of personnel have special abilities or facility synergies they can use when certain conditions are met. PCs don’t need to be present in the base for these abilities to function, but they might not reap the benefits until they return.

**Upgrade**

Some types of personnel have an upgrade option, which allows the PCs to hire a more skilled or powerful version of that type by paying a higher monthly rate. If hiring for multiple openings for a specific type, the PCs can choose to hire a mix of standard and upgraded versions. If the PCs have retained a standard version for at least a month, they can choose to later upgrade them rather than replace them. Upgrading in this way reflects an individual’s growth and improvement over time.

### Creating Custom Personnel

If the players find that none of the listed types of personnel meet their specific needs. The following guidance can help a GM create a new type, including its cost, prerequisites, upgrades, and any special abilities.

First, figure out what their primary duty will be. In some cases, it might be easiest to take an existing personnel type and modify it. However, if none makes sense, create a new one by considering the following along with their primary duty.

***Prerequisite.*** Most personnel types have at least one facility associated with them. What primary facility will the new type be working in? This is likely their prerequisite. For example, you can’t hire a shopkeep without a shop.

***Upgrades.*** In some cases, personnel types have the ability to be upgraded to function more efficiently or perform for a higher cost. This is rare and generally only makes sense for those whose primary abilities are tied to their stat block. For example, the arcanist can be upgraded from the **mage’s apprentice** to the **mage** stat block since crafting magical items and spell scrolls is directly tied to the strength of their spellcasting.

***Special Abilities.*** Once you know the new personnel type’s primary duty and any possible upgrades, consider if they have any special abilities. This is rare and typically requires solid justification.

***Cost.*** Once you know the parameters of the new personnel type, a price can be set. This depends on their skill set, stats, and any special abilities. Those that are highly specialized or conduct illegal activities for the PCs naturally cost far more than others.

## Mysteries

A base doesn’t spring into being out of nowhere. The best bases have a history all their own, though much of that history is buried by time, hidden by dust, damage, neglect, or previous inhabitants. When the PCs acquire a base, the GM should consider the property’s past and determine what kind of mysteries it holds.

Mysteries can take many forms, but their primary characteristic is their secret nature. PCs can only uncover these mysteries through direct interaction with the base— by spending significant time using its facilities or investing significant resources into its expansion. A mystery could take the form of a derelict facility buried in a partially collapsed wing of the base. It could be unidentified remains hidden within a wall. Or it could be a cache of gems hidden by a criminal on the run.

### Uncovering Mysteries

Since mysteries are hidden, the PCs must discover them. The easiest way to do this is through the improvement and expansion of their base. Maybe, in renovating a dusty chamber, they find something hidden beneath the floorboards. Maybe, in preparing a field for planting, they stumble on forgotten ruins within the dirt. Or maybe there’s an entire workshop already in the attic of a crumbling tower.

PCs can also discover mysteries by exploring their base more thoroughly. Taking the time to explore the base and its grounds for at least a day, plus a successful DC 18 INT (Investigation) check, might uncover such a mystery (GM’s discretion). Alternatively, the GM can decide that a PC finds it automatically simply by searching or else when it makes the most narrative sense for the mystery to be uncovered.

### Mysteries as Story Hooks

While mysteries can be simple rewards for base expansion and exploration, a mystery can also be a rich point for story expansion. It might provide clues as to the base’s previous inhabitants, it might be a hook to start a new quest, or it might even hide its own secrets that need to be unraveled.

#### Abandoned Mineshaft

An abandoned mineshaft can exist anywhere on the property but is most likely to be found in the more overgrown outdoor areas of the grounds. It can be horizontal and cut into a hillside or mountain, or it can be vertical and descend straight down into the earth. It might include a ladder or tracks for a mine cart.

To determine what lies within the mineshaft, the GM can roll on the **Mineshaft Properties** table or choose the result that works best for the campaign. More than one result can be included in the same mine for a more robust experience.

***Dungeon.*** The mineshaft leads to a dungeon. The complexity and inhabitants of the dungeon are up to the GM. It could be as simple as a small band of goblins or as challenging as a long-slumbering ancient dragon.

***Hidden Cache.*** Within the mine, the PCs find hidden treasure (see **Treasure Cache**).

***Secret Tunnel.*** The mineshaft leads to a tunnel that eventually leads off the property and ends somewhere else, like the sewers of a nearby town, the basement of a neighbor’s house, or deeper into a subterranean society. The PCs can repurpose a secret tunnel and convert it into an escape tunnel (see **Facilities**) for a quarter of the associated cost and time. Who installed this mineshaft— and why—is another mystery entirely.

***Untapped Vein.*** The mineshaft leads to a rich vein. It could be copper, gold, silver, platinum, iron, precious stones, or something more fantastic. But what to do with it once discovered is another story.

#### Forgotten Grave

This mystery might be an overgrown cemetery or mausoleum, an unmarked grave, or even bodies bricked up in the walls of the base itself. When adding an unexpected grave to a base, the GM can roll on the **Forgotten Grave Properties** table or choose the result that works best for the campaign.

***Bricked Up.*** A body is found within the walls of the base, hidden there by a previous inhabitant. It might be the remains of an enemy, a spouse, or someone else entirely. If the former inhabitant is still around, the discovery poses a potential problem for them. If they are friendly with the PCs, they might approach them and attempt to explain, but a hostile NPC might take matters into their own hands if they find out.

***Hidden Mausoleum.*** A stone mausoleum is found on the grounds of the base. It holds the ancestors of a family that used to reside there. Its doors are locked: the lock can be picked with a successful DC 15 DEX (Thieves’ Tools) check or forced with a successful DC 20 STR (Athletics) check. Each body lies within a stone coffin. The mausoleum might also hold additional mysteries, such as hidden treasure (see **Treasure Cache**).

***Overgrown Cemetery.*** A collection of headstones was covered by brush and other overgrowth and have only now been uncovered. The cemetery itself might only be a handful of graves, or it might be an expansive yard with dozens upon dozens of markers. Either way, the graves are old, listing names of previous inhabitants of the base or those from a nearby town or village.

***Unmarked Grave.*** While clearing land or digging a foundation for a base expansion or facility installation, a body is discovered. There is no grave marker nor any identifying information. A successful DC 15 WIS (Medicine) check reveals the cause of death. Identifying the body is more difficult, and the PCs might have to research the history of the previous inhabitants, question nearby villagers, or otherwise investigate to discover the answer to this puzzle.

*Forgotten Grave Properties*

| d4 | Grave | d4 | Grave |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Bricked Up | 3 | Overgrown Cemetery |
| 2 | Hidden Mausoleum | 4 | Unmarked Grave |

#### Immortal Prisoner

There is a prisoner hidden away somewhere in the base or on the grounds. This might be a vampire, locked in a mausoleum. Or a noble or royal, under the effects of the *[[7th-Circle Spells#Sequester|sequester]]* spell (see *Player’s Guide*), which might have been broken accidentally by the PCs. Or an infamous villain of lore, subjected to the *[[Rituals 7th–9th Circle#Imprisonment|imprisonment]]* spell (see *Player’s* *Guide*) and then lost to memory. The GM should determine who this individual is, what circumstances led to their imprisonment, and what must be done to free them, if anything can be done.

#### Locked Journal

This journal’s appearance is up to the GM. For example, it might be small and unassuming, or it might be made of rich leather and embossed with strange glyphs. Whatever its appearance, it is warded with a modified *[[Rituals 1st–3rd Circle#Arcane Lock|arcane lock]]* spell and a modified *[[Rituals 7th–9th Circle#Symbol|symbol]]* spell (see *Player’s Guide* for both spells). Opening the journal requires a successful DC 30 STR check, and once opened, each PC within 10 feet of it must succeed on a DC 25 WIS save or become frightened as per the fear effect of the *[[Rituals 7th–9th Circle#Symbol|symbol]]* spell. When dropped, the journal slams shut of its own accord. Alternatively, a PC can speak the password (assigned by the GM) aloud, temporarily disabling the effects of both spells and allowing the journal to be opened and read freely.

The GM should determine the author (likely a previous inhabitant) and the contents of the journal. It might be encoded, or its contents might be concealed with magic, requiring additional work from the PCs before they can read it. It might also hint at other mysteries that have yet to be discovered within the base or its grounds or else contain answers to previously discovered mysteries.

#### Magic Mirror

This mirror is massive—15-feet tall and 10-feet wide— and is obscured behind dusty velvet drapes. It might have ornate scrollwork in wood or metal, or it might be deceptively plain.

But it also acts as a portal to elsewhere. This could be a pocket dimension, a different realm of existence, a different location on the same plane, or any number of places. It might always be active or else require a command word or particular circumstances, like the light of the full moon (GM’s discretion).

It can be moved without affecting the magic that enchants it. However, if removed from the base, it ceases to function until returned.

#### Mystic Spring

The spring bubbles forth somewhere on the grounds of the base. Perhaps the previous inhabitants built a rudimentary or elaborate well around the source, befitting its qualities, or it might be in its natural state, undiscovered before now. Once discovered, the spring can be left undeveloped, enclosed, or even incorporated into a facility, such as a bathhouse, chapel, or infirmary.

The spring has strange, magical properties to it. To determine these properties, as well as any unusual features, the GM can roll twice on the **Spring Properties** table or choose the results that work best for the campaign. The first roll is for the spring’s unusual feature, and the second is for its property, which affects a creature that drinks the water. Once affected by the spring’s properties, it can’t be again for 24 hours or until the effects of an ongoing property end (whichever is longer). If water is taken from the spring, it loses its properties after 24 hours.

#### Overgrown Hedge Maze

This elaborate hedge maze might be hidden behind a tall garden wall or simply be disguised by the overgrowth of the neglected property. The maze might be completely mundane, or it might have magical properties. The GM can roll on the **Hedge Maze Properties** table or choose the result that works best for the campaign.

#### Ruins

Much like the PCs are not the first inhabitants of the base, the base itself might not be the first structure to exist on this particular plot of land. Ruins might be found while tilling a field for planting, while expanding an abandoned mineshaft, or while exploring the property. The GM can roll on the **Ruins Properties** table or choose the result that works best for the campaign. The ruins could potentially be restored or even incorporated into a new facility (GM’s discretion). Ruins might simply be an interesting curiosity that hints at previous civilizations, or they might hold clues to a greater mystery or even quest hooks for the PCs to pursue.

***Derelict Facility.*** The ruins could also have once housed a working facility, though it has ceased to be functional due to damage, decay, or neglect. These are typically discovered when PCs decide to improve upon existing, unusable spaces within their base. Repairing a derelict facility costs half the gold and time that would be required to install a new facility of the same type.

#### Secret Cellar

This subterranean stone room might have been lost for many reasons. Perhaps it was bricked up because of a rat infestation. Perhaps the original building above it was razed while its cellar faded from memory. Perhaps it’s set in a hillside, the entrance hidden by overgrown foliage. It miraculously still contains wine but might also contain other mysteries, such as hidden treasure (see **Treasure Cache**) or unexpected remains (see **Forgotten Grave**).

To determine the quality of the wine found within the cellar, the GM can roll on the **Wine Cellar Properties** table or choose the result that works best for the campaign. Multiple types of wine can be found within.

*Wine Cellar Properties*

| 4 | 20 bottles of fine wine worth 10 gp each and 10 barrels of fine wine worth 300 pp each. |

#### Sinister Shrine

The size of the shrine can vary widely, from a small altar that fits easily in a closet to a sprawling cathedral concealed behind a false wall or in a subterranean cavern. It is dedicated to a dark god, demon lord, archdevil, or other evil entity, and it typically features a statue, effigy, or symbol of that entity. Old offerings of a kind that would be attractive to the entity might be strewn about, left by a previous devotee. It might have been built by an individual worshiping in secret, or it might be a sign or a much larger cult presence.

The area of the shrine is considered desecrated against celestials and has one other effect bound to it, as per the *[[Rituals 4th–6th Circle#Hallow|hallow]]* spell (see *Player’s Guide*, GM’s discretion). PCs can rid the area of the desecration with appropriate magic. In addition, if the shrine is removed for a year, the desecration and its associated effect end.

#### Toadstool Ring

A ring of pale, glowing mushrooms exists somewhere in the base or on the grounds. It could be in a basement, a forest glade, a dry hillock in a swamp, or even an unused attic. The ring has a 10-foot diameter.

If the toadstools are destroyed, they regrow in 1d4 days. They grow in the previous location regardless of what else is placed there, including wood, stone, or metal. If the PCs install a facility, the toadstools spring up within the facility in the same spot. If the PCs install something that might ultimately displace the toadstools, like a body of water, the GM determines where the toadstools grow instead.

To determine the effect of the toadstool ring, the GM can roll on the **Toadstool Ring Properties** table or choose the result that works best for the campaign. Alternatively, the ring might have no strange properties other than its eerie persistence.

*Toadstool Ring Properties*

| d4 | Property |
|---|---|
| 1 | A creature that sleeps within the toadstool ring has prophetic dreams. |
| 2 | Fey creatures can effortlessly pass through the toadstool ring to and from the faerie realms, as if it were a gateway. |
| 3 | Stepping into the toadstool ring at a certain time can spirit a creature to the faerie realms or other location. |
| 4 | The mushrooms can be used to make certain poisons (GM’s discretion, see Game Master’s Guide and Player’s Guide for more on poisons). |

#### Treasure Cache

A treasure cache is a collection of coins, gemstones, or art that has monetary and possibly sentimental value. A cache could be found in a walled-up room, under the floorboards, buried somewhere on the grounds, or even enclosed within another mystery (such as a **Forgotten Grave)**.

To determine the type of cache found, the GM can roll on the **Treasure Cache** table or simply choose a result. More than one treasure cache can be found together or else alongside an arcane cache (see **Arcane Cache**). Additional treasure tables can be found in the *Game Master’s Guide*.

***Arcane Cache.*** The cache contains magic items instead of mundane treasures. To determine the contents, the GM can roll on the **Arcane Cache** table or simply choose a result. More than one treasure cache can be found together. For the table’s purposes, “consumable magic items” refers to magic items like potions and scrolls while “magic items” refers to any nonconsumable magic item, such as armor, weapons, shields, jewelry, staves, wands, and wondrous items. Magic item descriptions can be found in the *Player’s Guide*, and additional treasure bundles can be found in the *Game Master’s Guide*.
