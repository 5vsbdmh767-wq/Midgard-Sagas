*Optional, from Book of Blades.*

The methods and motivations of naval battles are well‑established in real-world history and can be easily adapted to create exciting maritime stories for your characters and campaigns.

## How Do You Fight?

During the Age of Sail, naval battles were dominated by gaining position with the wind, called holding the weather gauge, and using cannons to pummel the enemy. With limited exceptions of small chaser guns, cannons fire from the side of a vessel, meaning ships are vulnerable from the front or the rear. Whether your campaign uses black powder weapons, magical artillery, or traditional siege weapons like ballista, you can still use those ideas in your encounters.

The aft of the ship is especially vulnerable since the rudder is mounted there and a hit on the rear of the ship can cripple a ship’s ability to steer. When using cannons or similar siege weapons, there were two main doctrines on how to engage with the enemy during a battle.

***Target the Hull.*** The first doctrine, popularized by the British, was to target enemy ships at or below the waterline. Ruptured hulls took on water, slowing the damaged ship and requiring more crew to man pumps to prevent the ship from sinking. The disadvantage to this choice is that ships are made of surprisingly durable oak. Not only does oak resist damage, but it floats well. A damaged ship can toss spare sails, spars, supplies, or even extra ammunition over the side to raise its profile in the water.

***Target the Sails.*** The second doctrine, favored by the French, was to target enemy sails and rigging to take away a foe’s ability to maneuver and defend itself. A ship without its masts or sails can’t move, and an attacker can maneuver into a position where the enemy can’t fire back or easily run away. The disadvantage to this method is that most ships carry a lot of extra sail, rope, and spars. Temporary repairs to sails are easily made.

Both methods aim to slow a ship down till the attacker can maneuver into a superior position and demand surrender under threat of the attacker’s guns. With no way to fight back, such ships have little choice.

## Why Do You Fight?

It was not uncommon for sailors to sail under flags different from their homeland, leading to diverse crews. During the Age of Sail, navies frequently made up for their losses by taking skilled mariners from defeated ships. As long as the sailors were treated well by their officers and earned enough gold, they rarely cared whose face was stamped on the coins they were paid with.

So, if most sailors don’t care why they fight, why do ships do battle? There are generally three reasons that ships come into conflict.

***Controlling a Trade Route.*** Protecting a trade route or threatening the trade route of a rival is economic warfare turned hot. By sinking or raiding their ships, a navy reduces their enemy’s power to trade. They lose money. They are unable to resupply their forces. Their trade partners stop trusting them. Eventually, their finances are completely crippled. Governments frequently sponsored privateers for this purpose, creating a class of state-sanctioned pirates who could safely sell their stolen cargo back to their sponsoring government.

***Controlling Access to Land.*** In addition to damaging trade, ships might also battle to control a landing. Whether attacking a foe’s port from the sea or protecting a deepwater bay where troops or supplies can be offloaded, ships project force to distant lands. Without soldiers, you can’t control a new land, and without ships, you can’t get the soldiers there. A quick way to prevent an enemy from threatening you is to prevent their soldiers from ever arriving by sinking their transports.

***Piracy.*** Of course, the most storied reason for attacking another ship is to get rich. Merchant ships are slow, not well armed, and full of valuable cargo. While pirates want the cargo, the opposing ship itself is just as valuable a target for pirates and navies alike. If the ship is damaged but can be saved, minimum repairs are made, and it is brought back to a friendly port where the ship is sold and the prize money is divided.

A typical split is one-quarter of the bounty for the captain, one-eighth for commissioned officers like lieutenants, midshipmen, or the surgeon, another eighth for warrant officers or specialists like the boatswain, sailing master, gunner, or chaplain, and the remaining half split among the rest of the crew. Taking a prize earns enough wealth to change a sailor’s life.

## Losing The Fight

When a ship is defeated in combat, enemy sailors are usually rescued from the water or sinking ships. Executing prisoners or leaving sailors to drown almost never happens, and only then by the most despicable pirate crews seeking to cultivate a fearsome reputation. One of three fates typically awaits the surviving sailors of the defeated ship.

***Pressed into Service.*** As mentioned above, sailors of a defeated ship are typically captured and given the chance to join the opposing naval or pirate crew.

***Imprisoned and Ransomed.*** If they refuse, sometimes captives are imprisoned, or in the case of high-ranking sailors with rich families, ransomed home under the promise that they don’t take up arms in the current conflict.

***Marooned.*** Intentionally abandoning sailors on a tropical island is less common than stories might suggest and is a punishment reserved for officers who fail to put down a mutiny or as a punishment for pirates who cheat their crewmates. While some marooned sailors manage to survive and adapt to their new surroundings, marooning is usually a fatal punishment.

## Setting Sail

The **Tales of the Valiant** *Player’s Guide* and *Game Master’s Guide* contain rules for vehicle movement and combat. The alternate naval combat rules presented here are intended to make the ship a dynamic and exciting part of any conflict, with a little more complexity. While these rules refer to wind and sails, they can also be applied easily to navigating currents in the aether or using magical engines to surf the astral plane.

### Movement

Use the Speed statistic provided with the vehicle. If you draw from another source and the only speed provided for a vehicle is in miles per hour, a handy approximation of the number of feet the ship moves per round is to take the value given for the vessel’s miles per hour and multiply it by 10. This assumes the ship is being piloted. For example, a galley moves at 4 mph with average winds, or 40 ft. per round. If you are playing on a grid, round to the nearest multiple of 5 when counting squares. Without a shipboard action taken by the master of tops (see **Shipboard Actions** in this chapter), a ship may not Dash.

### Changing Direction

Sailing vessels don’t quickly turn. A ship changing direction without completing a shipboard action moves the first half of its speed in the direction it is facing and rotates 45° before moving the second half of its movement. For example, a turning galley would move 20 ft. in the direction it began the round, and then another 20 ft. at a 45° diagonal to its previous course.

### Direction of Wind and Drifting

The direction of the wind is of critical importance to sailing a ship, determining if it can move easily in a straight line or is forced to tack back and forth. These rules simplify the importance of wind direction assuming that the crew is tacking and making other appropriate adjustments to the sails. If a ship is not controlled, it drifts in the direction of the wind: 5 feet for light wind, 10 feet for average wind, and 15 feet for strong wind.

### Turning Into Irons

The one direction ships never want to go is directly into the wind. A vessel that turns into the wind and tries to move in that direction has its speed decreased by the drift distance listed above, at the end of each round. If the ship’s speed is greater than 0 ft., the helmsman must succeed on a DC 12 INT (Water Vehicles) check to turn the ship. If the helmsman fails to turn the ship or the ship’s speed was reduced to 0 ft., it continues to drift in the direction of the wind and is stuck until the wind changes.

### Wind Speed

Distance traveled assumes average wind speeds. Over the course of days, this is likely to be the case. During a combat round however, that doesn’t have to be the case. If there is no wind, sailing ships don’t move. If there are light winds, halve the ship’s speed, and if there are strong winds, double it.

## Crew Quality

While skilled, standard crew members can be hired for 2 gp per member per day, what about non-standard crew? For extra coin, or even a share of the booty, better crews can be hired and relied upon. Amateur or disreputable sailors can be brought on for less pay, but they don’t perform their jobs as well and are more likely to mutiny. An expert crew shows mastery in one area, while an amateur crew has one area where they are a liability to the characters. This mastery or liability carries a multiplier applied to the base cost. For example, an expert crew has a cost multiplier of ×2, so it costs 4 gp per member per day to hire them, while a clumsy amateur crew has a cost multiplier of ×1/2, so it costs 1 gp per member per day to hire them. Consult the **Hired Crew Quality** table for a full list of available masteries and liabilities, their associated costs, and special properties.

## Crew Member Stat Blocks

For most fights, you generally don’t need to track each crew member’s hit points or abilities. Instead, only roll for a crew member when specifically called for. If you are taking time to track your ship’s crew, it might be worthwhile to design unique stat blocks for all the named NPCs. However, quick baselines are probably more feasible for a GM. The average sailor has the statistics of a **bandit**. A quick stat block for an officer could be a **noble** or a **scout**, while the captain might use a **spy** or **bandit captain** stat block.

## Loyalty And Mutiny

The possibility of a crew mutinying against its officers is a real threat at sea, especially if the crew is treated poorly. There are several ways the GM might track this danger. The first is simply to decide when and if it happens. If the PCs treat their crew poorly, the crew responds accordingly. This story-driven method is likely easiest for GMs since it involves no tracking.

A second method is to check for mutiny each time the crew feels slighted or loses faith in their leaders. When this occurs, the PC acting as captain or boatswain must make a CHA (Intimidation or Persuasion) ability check modified by any mastery or liability (see the Effects column on the **Hired Crew Quality** table). On a success, the crew is quieted, but any further mutiny checks required until the crew has taken a long rest are at an increased DC. On a failure, the crew becomes completely unmanageable and might attack the officers. All ship movement stops, and no shipboard actions are available until the captain or boatswain takes some extraordinary measure to regain the crew’s loyalty (as determined by the GM).

The GM sets the DC of this check based on the severity of the precipitating incident. The **Typical Difficulty Class** table in **Chapter 6** of *Player’s Guide* has the standard DCs for tasks.

## Prize Pay

It is common practice to sell captured ships and distribute the money among the crew. Letting your crew share in the spoils reinforces their loyalty, giving you advantage on CHA checks for 1 month when you need to test them.

If you take a prize but don’t reward the crew, you have disadvantage for 1 month in the same checks. Sailors will put up with a lot when their pockets are full.

## Weather

Traveling by sea in a fantasy world, or even magically in the aether, means encountering strange weather in addition to the normal variations of wind and visibility. *Game Master's Guide* briefly touches on weather, but it does not include the detail one might expect for ships fighting in the sea and sky, or even stranger places.

Use the **Wind** and **Precipitation** tables here to determine how the sailing fares. Effects on movement assume the ship is powered by sails. If it is magically powered or derives its power from some other source, an effect might not be relevant.

## Naval Combat

A ship-to-ship battle can be a thrilling addition to your adventure. Here are some additional rules to help you create electrifying ship combats.

## Crew Positions

Traditional sailing ships have dozens of different positions, but these rules simplify them to just a few so PCs can take them on and hire crew for the rest (see the **Hired Crew Quality** table). At a minimum, a sailing ship needs the following manned stations:

- ***Boatswain.*** The boatswain, or “bosun,” relays orders and decides who will actually carry them out. While the captain may be a god on high, the bosun is the stout fist the officers deploy to make sure the crew does what they are supposed to. The bosun is one of the most experienced sailors on the ship and can substitute for any position as needed.

- ***Helm.*** The crew member at the helm steers the ship. Without a helm, the ship is uncontrolled.

- ***Master of Tops.*** The master of tops and the rest of the riggers control the sails and the ship’s speed.

Many other positions can be filled on ship, but not all are needed, especially during a fight. Optional crew stations include the following:

- ***Captain.*** The captain chooses the course, picks targets, and holds the crew together by strength of will. If a captain goes down in combat, other officers can step up and fill the position. For the sake of shipboard actions, any officer might fill this position. It takes the captain an action to redistribute crew to fill stations lost to casualties.

- ***Carpenter.*** The master carpenter and mates are responsible for maintaining the ship and making repairs. Sailmakers are technically a separate specialty, but for the sake of ease, they are lumped together with carpenters for shipboard actions.

- ***Cook.*** The crew can’t fight if they are starving, and morale suffers if they’re forced to eat poor food. A good cook is beloved by the crew.

- ***Gunner.*** The master gunner and the rest of the gunnery crew maintain and fire the weapons. There is a group of sailors responsible for each weapon aboard ship. Gunners are needed if the ship is to fight back and not simply run.

- ***Purser.*** The purser is responsible for money matters, especially for paying the crew. On small ships, the purser and quartermaster might be the same person.

- ***Quartermaster.*** The quartermaster maintains and stows supplies and does the recordkeeping. The quartermaster works closely with the purser, and sometimes they are the same person for small crews.

- ***Surgeon.*** Injuries are common aboard a ship. An estimated two-thirds of sailors might die or become severally injured on most journeys, so having a trained surgeon to nurse as many back to health is key to success. During a combat, the surgeon works to save as many crew lives as possible.

## Initiative

Characters and unique foes not concerned with running a ship roll initiative as normal. Ships move last in initiative, and their station crews act on their ship’s turn in any order. However, determine initiative between competing vessels each round. Each round, to decide which ship acts first, determine which ship has the advantage of position by having each helm make a WIS (Water Vehicles) check. The winner chooses whether to act first or delay. If the helm has no one assigned, that ship automatically fails the check.

## Damaging The Ship

A weapon is staffed by the number of crew needed to fire it once per round. For example, a ballista takes one action to load and one action to fire, so a ballista would be crewed by two sailors, one of which might be the master gunner.

If an enemy is in the firing arc of a weapon, generally 90° from the direction it is mounted, a gunnery crew can attempt to fire on it. When attacking a ship, the lead gunner makes an attack roll against the opposing ship’s AC. On a hit, it deals damage as usual. This assumes the gunner is targeting the ship itself and damaging the hull as opposed to attempting to damage a specific part of the enemy vessel. If there are strong winds, the ship’s gunner has disadvantage to hit their target due to rough seas. Consult the **Damage to the Ship** table for the effects of damage to the hull.

**Damage to the Ship**

| % of Ship's HP Lost | Effect |
|---|---|
| 25% | The ship has a rupture in its hull and is taking on water. The ship’s speed decreases by 5 ft. |
| 50% | The ship has a second rupture in its hull and is taking on even more water. The ship’s speed decreases by 10 ft. |
| 75% | The ship has a third rupture in its hull and is slowly sinking. The ship’s speed decreases by 15 ft. Unless the master carpenter begins repairs, the ship will sink in 2d6 rounds. |
| 100% | The ship has been holed and can no longer move or maneuver. It is sinking. Unless the master carpenter begins repairs, the ship will sink in 1d4 rounds. |

## Targeting Crew And Casualties

Gunners don’t usually use ship weapons against enemy sailors. They’re too small and nimble to make that effective. That said, PCs or marines might pick their targets as normal, or sailors might be injured as collateral damage from an attack. When a gunner scores a critical hit, in addition to rolling the damage dice twice, determine the location of the ship that was hit using the Disable Station shipboard action (see **Shipboard Actions**).

Roll a d6 to determine how many crew members in that area are unconscious and dying. If a PC surgeon, cleric, or similar NPC does not take action to save them, 75 percent of them die. If a PC is in the area hit, they take half the damage done by the attack from flying shrapnel. If the PC succeeds on a DEX save with a DC equal to the enemy gunner’s attack roll, they instead take no damage from shrapnel.

If you desire, you can track the number of crew members at each station and require the captain to take an action to redeploy crew members to specific stations. Stations without sufficient crew have disadvantage to all checks and take an additional round to perform shipboard actions. Stations without any functioning crew can’t take actions. For a simpler system, apply a 10-foot movement penalty for every five crew members that are killed or disabled on a ship with 20 or more crew, or every two crew members on a ship with fewer than 20 crew.

## Shipboard Actions

During a naval engagement, PCs can choose to act as they would in any other combat. Throwing fireballs or raining down a volley of arrows remain effective tactics! Note however, that most engagements begin at (2d6 + 2) × 100 feet, so PC attacks are not generally in range when engagements commence.

PCs can fill a role in the ship’s crew or help as emergencies arise. PCs can choose the order in which a ship’s stations act, with the following shipboard actions available. In almost all cases, these are Use an Object actions.

### All Stop

*Crew Position: Master of Tops* The master of tops attempts a DC 12 DEX (Water Vehicles) check to strike the sails and drop the anchor, bringing the ship’s speed to 0 feet after it completes its movement this round. If the check fails, the ship continues moving for another round before the anchor catches. This check can’t be attempted in deep water.

### Come About

*Crew Position: Helm* The helm attempts a severe change of direction with a DC 15 DEX (Water Vehicles) check. If successful, the ship turns 90° instead of the normal 45°. If the check fails, the ship doesn’t turn at all. This check has disadvantage if it crosses against the direction of the wind.

### Disable Station

*Crew Position: Gunner* The gunner fires to disable stations on the enemy vessel. This attack has disadvantage. If the attack hits, instead of doing damage to the hull, one station is damaged according to the **Disable Station Effects** table. Roll a d6 to determine how many crew members in that area are unconscious and dying. Any PCs in the area take half the damage unless they succeed on a DEX save with a DC equal to the enemy gunner’s attack roll, in which case they personally take no damage from the attack.

### Escape a Grapple

*Crew Position: Boatswain* The boatswain leads a number of crew in cutting lines that tie their vessel to another. A successful DC 13 CHA (Intimidation or Persuasion) check, in concert with a number of crewmembers equal to one per 10-foot length of the ship, allows everyone to work together to cut the ropes and allow the vessel to maneuver normally. This shipboard action takes 1 additional round to complete for every two crew members below the required number. On a failed check, the action takes 1 additional round to complete.

### Evasive Maneuver

*Crew Position: Helm* The helm makes an INT (Water Vehicles) check contested by the enemy helmsman. On a success, enemy gunners have disadvantage to hit the ship.

### Force Position

*Crew Position: Helm* The helm makes a contested INT (Water Vehicles) check opposed by the enemy helm to force the enemy into a disadvantageous position. On a success, the enemy ship has disadvantage on its WIS (Water Vehicles) check to determine initiative next round. ON a failure, the enemy gains advantage to its initiative check next round.

### Full Speed Ahead

*Crew Position: Master of Tops* The master of tops uses its action to adjust the sails and gain a burst of speed. The ship can Dash this turn. If the ship is powered by oars, the rowers (make one roll for the group) must succeed on DC 12 CON save or gain one level of exhaustion.

### Grapple

*Crew Position: Boatswain* The boatswain leads a number of crew in throwing grappling hooks onto an enemy vessel within 5 feet and tying the ships together. While tied, neither ship may move or maneuver. This action requires a successful DC 13 CHA (Intimidation or Persuasion) check and a number of crewmembers equal to one per 10-foot length of the enemy ship. It takes 1 additional round to complete for every two crew members below the required number. If the check fails, the action fails, and the other ship can move normally. Jumping from one grappled ship to another requires a successful DC 10 STR (Athletics) check. If this check is failed by 5 or more, the sailor falls into the water.

### Hold Her Steady

*Crew Position: Helm* The helm holds the ship steady, giving its gunnery crews advantage to hit one enemy ship. All enemy ships gain advantage to hit the ship holding steady.

### Motivate

*Crew Position: Boatswain or Captain* As a reaction, the officer shouts orders to the crew in the middle of some shipboard action. On a successful DC 13 CHA (Intimidation or Persuasion) check, the officer gives advantage on a specific check that an NPC crew member makes.

### Patch

*Crew Position: Master Carpenter* The carpenter leads a damage repair crew of four other crew members to put in a temporary patch on the hull or repair a damaged station. A successful WIS (Carpenter’s Tools) check restores 2d6 hit points to the ship or restores a station to working order. This action can only be used once per hole or damaged station. The DC to restore hit points to the ships is 11 + 2 per hole the ship has suffered, and DC 15 to repair a station. It takes 1 additional round to complete this repair for every two crew members below the five required.

### Ram

*Crew Position: Helm* If moving with the wind, the helm can attempt to ram another vessel by making a successful DEX (Water Vehicles) check against the other ship’s AC. If successful, both ships take 4d10 damage for every size category of the ramming ship above Tiny. If the ship is equipped with a ram, it takes only half damage. Both ships become hopelessly grappled and can’t be separated without several minutes of work. If the attack missed, the ships graze and both take one-quarter damage. You can’t take the Ram shipboard action against the direction of the wind.

### Sheer

*Crew Position: Helm* Only effective against ships with oars, the helm brings their vessel within 5 feet of the enemy vessel, sheering off all the oars on one side of the enemy vessel. This brings its speed to 0 feet for 3 rounds until new oars can be deployed. To succeed, the helm must make a successful DEX (Water Vehicles) check with a DC equal to the other ship’s AC.
