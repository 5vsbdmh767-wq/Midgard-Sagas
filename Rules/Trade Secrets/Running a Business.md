%% source: Trade Secrets %%

PCs with a more entrepreneurial nature might wish to start a business of their own, buying and selling whatever goods they feel are likely to make them the most profit. How and where they try this has an impact on not just their startup costs, but also on their ongoing operating costs, and ultimately, their profits. For choosing a location, trade, and inventory, see [[Creating a Business]].

## Ways to Build

The common ways to get started in business are through investing in someone else’s venture and, of course, establishing your own.

### Investing

Finding an existing business in need of a little more capital is an easy way to set up passive income. Whatever money the PCs decide to contribute is added to the business’s Stake (see [[#Running a Business]]). The players and the GM should keep track of what percentage of the total Stake the PCs’ investment comes to.

After a year in-game, the GM rolls on the **Commerce Resolution** table to determine whether the business prospered, lost, or broken even in the previous year. If the business did well, the PC investors receive a percentage of the profit, based on the percentage of the original Stake provided.

#### Calculating the Stake

To calculate how much the investor is entitled to at the end of the investment interval, use the formula below:

**(Ending Stake − Starting Stake) × PC percentage = Payout**

So, for example, if the PC investment represents 25% of the business’s initial Stake, the PCs are entitled to 25% of the profits at the end of the year. (Most business partners prefer their investors to reinvest profits into the business, but that decision is up to the players.)

It’s also possible that a business loses money. In that case, the PCs have to decide whether to give their partner time to recoup their losses, invest additional money, or cash out (see [[#Going Out of Business]]).

### From the Ground Up

If the PCs want more challenge, they can build their business up from nothing more than a patch of land and some money. [[Setting Up Shop]] has more information on how to build a physical storefront, where it can be built, what it costs, and how it can be modified to improve the profits (or cut a few corners).

> [!note] Maximum Stake
> Your shop’s Stake mostly represents physical inventory, so your total Stake is limited by the size of your place of business. See [[Setting Up Shop]] for more information.

## Securing Supply

Whether you’re selling goods or services, you need a steady supply of stock to stay in business and keep your customers happy. You can do this by buying your inventory directly from the source, buying from a go-between, generating it yourself, or some combination of all three.

Arranging a steady supply from the source or an intermediary requires that you pay their asking price. However, market conditions, particularly competitors, have an effect on that price. See the **Supply Acquisition** table for more information.

**Supply Acquisition**

| Market Competition | Price Adjustment |
|---|---|
| Monopoly: Supply is bought by one or more existing buyers. | +30% |
| High Competition: Demand is far higher than supply. | +20% |
| Seller’s Market: Demand mostly exceeds supply. | +10% |
| Major Interest: Demand is slightly greater than supply. | +5% |
| Balanced: The market has an equilibrium between supply and demand. | — |
| Minor Interest: Supply is slightly greater than demand. | −2% |
| Buyer’s Market: Supply far exceeds demand. | −5% |
| No Competition: The supplier’s product is not in demand in the area. | −10% |

### From the Source

Farmers, crafters, miners, and similar operations are primary sources for a shop’s inventory. They do the work of producing the sellable goods, then sell them to you at bulk pricing. You sell them to another merchant, or straight to the end user.

### From an Intermediary

Intermediaries usually buy stock from the source and transport it to the appropriate market, where they resell it at a profit. This category commonly includes trade caravans, shipping fleets, and even some guilds (if they negotiate standard prices on their members’ behalf).

### From Your Own Stock

The easiest way to secure supply is to provide it yourself, by spending some of your downtime crafting sellable items or by selling items you acquire through your own efforts.

## Hiring Staff

This section details all the different kinds of help you can hire, how they contribute to your profits and operations, and what you can expect to pay them.

For more information on how hiring staff affects your daily profits, see [[#Running a Business]].

### Hireling Pay Expanded

The section on [[Mounts & Services#Services|Services]] in the *Player’s Guide* describes the kinds of hirelings a PC might contract for short-term jobs: hauling loot, casting healing spells, tending the party’s camp and looking after their mounts. These are all the generally low-risk things that adventurers don’t have the time or the inclination to do for themselves.

Hirelings are rated as either “skilled,” for those with some basic proficiency, or “untrained” for everyone else. They get paid either 2 gp or 2 sp per day, respectively.

> [!note] Skilled vs. Unskilled Labor
> As explained in the *Player’s Guide*, the “skilled labor” category covers workers who have a proficiency from a skill, tool, vehicle, armor, or weaponry. This isn’t necessarily factored in to an NPC’s ability modifiers.
>
> Unskilled labor is virtually everyone else. Those workers do straightforward physical labor such as ditch diggers, porters, and tally clerks.
>
> If in doubt about whether a given worker is skilled or unskilled, consider what PB an average day of their work would require. If the result of their most-used ability check doesn’t really matter or isn’t necessary to roll, treat the laborer as untrained.

#### Hireling Skill Levels

The more skilled a hireling is, the more you must pay them. For most of the professions listed here, their level of skill can be considered the *starting* level. More experienced representatives of their craft or profession with a higher proficiency bonus are paid at a higher rate.

**Untrained** hirelings do the kind of work that most people can do: menial or repetitive work that requires no particular skill.

**Novices** are a step up from the untrained, because they’ve at least had to learn how to succeed at their assigned tasks with a few days practice or with skilled oversight.

**Apprentices** have had the most basic training in their field of expertise. They might be new to the profession or failed to advance beyond the basics.

**Journeymen** grasp the fundamentals of their craft and can apply those lessons to get a complex job done on their own.

**Experts** have mastered every skill of their profession, and they can apply what they’ve learned in precise, creative, and often original ways. They are so accomplished that they can teach others and lead large groups of less-skilled practitioners in major projects.

**Masters** are legends in their field and are the trailblazers who demonstrate advanced facility in every aspect of their profession—from training journeymen and experts, to running schools, to writing books on the subject. They often invent entirely new applications of their skills, new ways of working, or new materials and methods.

### Benefits of Hirelings

In addition to providing opportunities to use the [[Rolling the Dice#Working Together|Working Together]] and [[Rolling the Dice#Group Checks|Group Check]] rules (see the *Player’s Guide*), hirelings can supply proficiencies that the PC doesn’t have. This allows them to make checks that the PC would otherwise be unable to attempt.

Alternately, a hireling with an identical or complementary proficiency can step in for the PC on occasions when the call of adventure is stronger than the call of commerce. In such cases, the GM can make **Commerce Resolution** checks for the PC’s business, using the NPC hireling’s ability check bonus, as shown on the **Hireling Costs by Skill and Proficiency** table.

For most hirelings, find an appropriate stat block in the NPC sections of *Monster Vault* or a similar source. Add the value in the Ability Check Bonus column to rolls when the hireling is doing something job-related for you.

Hirelings on staff have an effect on your Stake, but note that unlike the physical elements of your shop (see [[Setting Up Shop]]), this effect only applies on those days when you pay the hireling. For example, if you pay a farmer hireling for a day’s work, your Stake for that day increases by 3%. If you pay a clerk for a day’s work, your Stake at the end of the day increases by 1%.

#### Hireling Descriptions

Here’s more detail about each kind of hireling.

***Animal Handler.*** This category includes shepherds, drovers, stable attendants, zookeepers, and anyone else whose primaries responsibilities consist of feeding animals, cleaning them, disposing of their waste, and keeping them safe from predators and thieves.

***Arcane Caster.*** This category includes bards, sorcerers, and wizards, who advise and assist on market matters directly related to arcana, such as buying and selling magic items, arcane spell components, and magical traditions.

***Appraiser.*** Appraisers are specialists whose job duties revolve around determining the value of items and objects. They often focus on a particular field, such as gems, jewelry, antiques, or magic items.

***Assembler.*** This type of hireling takes individual components and combines them to make something else. Examples include shoemakers, tailors, and carpenters.

***Attendant.*** A valet, groom, handmaiden, lady’s maid, or the like is an attendant.

***Builder.*** For more complex assembly projects, there are builders, who get paid to make structures out of wood, stone, bricks, cob, or whatever else is common for the area.

***Cleaner.*** This category covers workers who keep things looking shiny and new. It includes scullery staff, street sweepers, chimney sweeps, stable boys, house maids, and laundry workers.

***Clerk.*** Clerks are untrained labor, because their primary duties include keeping tallies, minding inventory, and keeping records up to date.

***Collector.*** This category includes bill collectors and tax collectors, and it also covers people who go to market on someone else’s behalf, or who find and sell junk. (Those who forage for items are gatherers.)

***Cook.*** This category includes cooks and chefs, as well as waiters—anyone who prepares food or serves it.

***Crafter.*** Perhaps the most common category, crafters turn raw materials into something that others want to buy, either as the end product, or as a component in their own craft: basket-weavers, brewers, distillers, tanners, smiths, coopers, wheelwrights, winemakers, and similar professions.

***Criminal.*** Although most people wouldn’t identify this work as their own profession, crime is a form of business, and one can hire criminals to perform tasks that more reputable hirelings wouldn’t have the least notion of how to pull it off. Of course, the listed daily pay is just for doing something that could get them arrested. Riskier endeavors must be negotiated separately.

***Divine Caster.*** This category includes clerics and paladins, who advise and assist on market matters directly related to the divine, including buying and selling magic items, divine spell components, holy symbols, and matters involving secret cults.

***Engineer.*** Engineers work closely with builders and both design the projects that will be under construction, and also solve the problems that always arrive, without simply throwing gold at them.

***Farmer.*** Those who till the soil to raise crops fall into the category of farmer. (Those who raise animals are ranchers.)

***Gatherer.*** Gatherers find raw materials in the wild for food preparation, including those who collect berries for dye and dung to burn in stoves. This profession might not use tools. A lumberjack, for example, needs more equipment that someone who gathers firewood from the forest floor.

***Sentry.*** Also known as a guard, a sentry protects you, your domicile, or your possessions. A sentry prevents thieves from leaving the premises, ejects troublemakers, and works to keep you safe if someone threatens violence.

***Healer.*** This category includes those who use simple treatments of wounds and illnesses, those who concoct herbal remedies, doctors, chirurgeons, and even magical means of healing.

***Hunter.*** A hunter locates wild animals and dispatches them efficiently and safely. Some hunt for food or usable parts, others hunt to keep predatory animals in check.

***Mender.*** Anyone who repairs things that are no longer functional or otherwise damaged counts as a mender, such as tinkers, seamsters, masons, or mechanists.

***Minder.*** This basic type of guard watches over a business and reports back, but can’t necessarily be counted on for stopping theft.

***Overseer.*** These hirelings lead others, often known as foremen, managers, or taskmasters. When you have an overseer, your other hirelings can make group checks (see *Player’s Guide*) for tasks such as completing projects and assisting customers. If half or more of the hirelings making the group check succeed, the attempt succeeds (or at least makes a day’s progress).

***Performer.*** Musicians, singers, dancers, actors, jesters, storytellers, acrobats, and more all fall into this category.

***Primordial Caster.*** The signature primordial spellcaster is the druid. A druid hireling advises and assists on market matters directly related to nature, such as buying and selling magic items, primordial spell components, plants, and animals.

***Processer.*** This category includes those who turn raw materials into something more desirable: millers, dyers, charcoal burners, and potters. The difference between this and a crafter is that a processor primarily sets the work into motion and stops it at the right time. A crafter remains hands-on throughout the entire operation.

***Ranch Hand.*** Ranch hands help raise animals to sell to butchers when the time is right.

***Servant.*** This category is a catch-all for untrained labor that is expected to perform whatever menial task is required of them.

***Subject Authority.*** This is a catch-all term for someone who’s achieved mastery of their field: farming, crafting, mining, or whatever a person can be excellent at. If you want to hire the very best in the field, this is your choice.

***Trader.*** Merchants, shopkeepers, tavernkeepers, and barmaids are all traders: those who sell goods to the public.

***Trapper.*** Different from a hunter, a trapper locates where animals are likely to be at some point in the future, and sets out a trap to capture or kill the animal, even when the trapper isn’t present.

***Transporter.*** Those who drive wagons, carts, carriages, boats, ships, or other vehicles, or carry goods by mule, camel, or horseback, are in the category of transporter.

***Veteran Guard.*** This type of guard has significant experience and is a proactive guardian of you and your property. A veteran guard takes steps to prevent theft, identifies and shores up weaknesses in your property and among staff, and goes to recover lost or stolen goods, even at personal risk.

***Warrior.*** Different from a sentry or other type of guard, a warrior can serve as an alert, highly competent guard. However, you can also direct a warrior to go do violence to your enemies elsewhere. They expect to be paid in advance for this type of work.

***Wyrd Caster.*** Warlocks and witches are wyrd spellcasters. They can advise and assist on market matters directly related to the supernatural, such as magic items, wyrd spell components, and eldritch symbols.

#### Hireling Loyalty

Normally, the people who work for you are not particularly loyal or disloyal. Attempts to persuade them to do something that isn’t in their immediate interests are ordinary CHA (Persuasion) checks.

If you pay hirelings slightly more than their listed rate—e.g., an extra coin of the default denomination—your CHA (Persuasion) checks have advantage. A hireling getting paid well is interested in keeping up the run. On the other hand, if you pay less than their usual rate (or haven’t paid them lately), those CHA (Persuasion) checks have disadvantage.

You can apply this same rule to any test of hireling morale, particular to matters of physical danger. Well paid hirelings are more likely to stick around.

## Going Out of Business

When the time comes to close up shop, you can sell your business to someone else or sell your remaining inventory at bargain prices. You could also walk away and let looters liquidate your stock for you, but let’s go with just the first two options.

### Selling Out

If you sell your business or your investment in it to someone else, you can make a DC 10 CHA (Persuasion) or (Deception) check to convince the buyer to pay what your percentage of the business is currently worth. That is, if you own 75% of a business that is currently worth 1,000 gp, you can receive 750 gp for your share.

If you succeed at this check by 5 or more, the potential buyer offers you 5 percent more than your share is currently worth. This increases to 10 percent if you succeed by 10 or more.

If you fail this check, however, the potential buyer offers you 10 percent less than your share is currently worth. If you fail by 5 or more, they offer you 25 percent less than your share’s value. You can only attempt this check once for each potential buyer.

### Cashing Out

Rather than selling your business to someone else, you can have one last sale to sell off all of your stock. In this case, make one final roll on the **Commerce Resolution** table, with whatever modifiers the GM deems are applicable. The result determines how much money you’re left with, and your shop’s shelves are now empty.

## Running a Business

*New downtime activity.* See [[Between Adventures]] for the general downtime rules.

You spend your downtime engaging in commerce. This requires not only buying and selling loot or other goods, but setting up a storefront. This could be a peddler’s box of wares, a stall in the public market, or a permanent shop in a town.

To select this activity, a PC must have the following:

1. A place to set up shop.

2. A supply of the goods or services on offer.

3. Customers willing and able to pay for the goods or services.

4. Enough coin, trade goods, or barter goods to serve as currency.

Work with your GM to establish commonsense limitations on where you can set up your business, and whether you have access to all your desired goods or services. The GM determines whether any customers are present who can afford the prices (and don’t try to steal from you).

***Time.*** To run a business, you must spend at least 1 day of downtime buying and/or selling goods or services.

***Cost.*** The cost of running a business is as variable as the goods or services being sold. Rather than providing an exhaustive list, work with the GM to determine a gp value of your stock, and consider this your base investment, or “Stake” for short.

Stake represents how much inventory and money you have on hand at the start of your business day. Depending on your business acumen, your Stake can increase, decrease, or stay the same.

### Converting Items to Inventory

A character running a business might want to add loot from their adventures to their inventory, selling it to the end consumer themselves, rather than taking it to a middle-man. In cases like this, you can take items out of your possessions and add half of their gp value to their Stake. You can only do this during downtime spent running a business.

This effectively turns the item into a number. For example, if a PC adds a *[[Magic Weapons#Trident of Fish Command|trident of fish command]]* to her inventory, she no longer has the trident. It’s now a 1,020 gp increase to her Stake.

### Converting Inventory to Items

If a character can convert items to inventory, it stands to reason that they can do the opposite: turning inventory into a specific item or items, which they can then add to their list of possessions. A PC can only do this once per day, during downtime spent running a business.

To do this, the PC must have enough gp in their Stake to cover the cost of the item or items in question. However, because this is their own stock, they can subtract half of the item’s gp value from their Stake.

To use the previous example, a PC running a business would need to have a Stake of at least 2,040 gp to convert a portion of their inventory into a *[[Magic Weapons#Trident of Fish Command|trident of fish command]]*. After doing so, she has the trident, and her Stake is reduced by 1,020 gp.

The GM can decide that some very rare or unique items aren’t available in this way. (*[[Magic Weapons#Holy Avenger|Holy avengers]]* are hard to come by.) See [[#What’s in Stock?]] for a more detailed system to determine what is or isn’t available.

#### What’s in Stock?

Your business might have a specific item in inventory provided that:

1. It’s appropriate to their usual wares,
2. The value of the item is less than or equal to their current Stake, and
3. The GM deems it reasonable that you have a reason to have it in stock but haven’t used it yet.

As a guideline, the GM can require that you spend 1 Luck to make a Wisdom check. The DC for this check is based on the gp value of the item, as shown in the **Stock Availability Difficulty** table. On a success, you have the item in your merchant inventory and can use it as you wish.

**Stock Availability Difficulty**

| Item Rarity | WIS DC |
|---|---|
| Common | 10 |
| Uncommon | 15 |
| Rare | 20 |
| Very Rare | 25 |
| Unique or Fabled | 30 |

***Resolution.*** At the end of each day spent running a business, the PC makes a single ability check using a skill or tool that the PC and GM agree is most relevant to the type of commerce conducted. Most often this will be CHA (Persuasion).

The check determines how much money the PC earned that day, as shown on the **Commerce Resolution** table. Monetary values assume a standard economy in a typical fantasy setting, but the GM should adjust these values as suits the needs of the game.

**Commerce Resolution**

| Check Result | Resolution |
|---|---|
| 1–5 | You lose 10% of your Stake for the day |
| 6–15 | You neither earn nor lose money for the day |
| 16–20 | You earn 10% of your Stake for the day |
| 21–25 | You earn 20% of your Stake for the day |
| 26+ | You earn 30% of your Stake for the day + bonus percentage equal to your PB × 3 |

Note that if you are using the [[Setting Up Shop]] rules, the choices you make there modify this roll, the percentages listed in the resolutions, or both.
