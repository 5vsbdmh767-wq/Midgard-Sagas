Before a business can operate, it needs someplace to conduct its business. This page covers financing a business, building it from components, choosing a location, and buying or renting a shop, workshop, factory, or farm. For the daily rules of making money, see [[Running a Business]].

To set up a business, you’ll need to decide where it will be located, how big it will be, how many people will work there, and most importantly, how you will pay for it. All of those factors, combined, determine how much trade you’ll do in your shop, and how that affects your daily profits. These rules dovetail with the section **New Downtime Activity: Running a Business** in **Chapter 1**.

## Financing

The money to start a business has to come from somewhere, and while the adventuring lifestyle can be highly lucrative, it’s not always reliable. Sometimes, it makes more sense to line up investors, find a patron, or sign up with a trade or craft guild.

What kind of funding you choose affects your Stake: the amount of money you start with at the beginning of each day of commerce. This mechanic is described in more detail under [[Running a Business]].

### Self-Funding

The obvious advantage of self-funding is that all the profits are yours to keep. The obvious drawback is that any losses are yours to suffer. And while it might seem reasonable that a PC can convert some adventuring loot into additional funding, it can become glaringly evident that pouring more money into a failing business is a losing proposition.

### Investors

Investors provide you with money to start your business because they expect to make more money later. In financial terms, a yearly return on investment of 5–7% is considered reasonable, but more is obviously better, and any profit that encourages the investor to put more money in (or at least not sell their interest) provides a safe position for a business owner.

Investors provide some of the business’s initial Stake and expect the business to repay them with a matching percentage of the business’s profits.

You find investors in the traditional way: by locating the wealthiest people around and asking them to fund your endeavor. Investors can be other, more successful merchants who are looking to fill a gap in their supply chain; nobles who want to turn their excess money into even more money (preferably without any real work on their part); government officials who are hoping to attract businesses that provide employment for workers and taxes for local coffers; and so on. Even more experienced adventurers could be investors, looking for a storefront to sell off various and sundry goods that they don’t have a use for. (Note that guild investment is a separate method, with its own mechanics.)

When you meet with potential investors, you must make a successful CHA (Persuasion) check to get them to hand over their money. You can choose to use CHA (Deception) instead, but if you fail, your dishonesty is not only revealed, but you also suffer disadvantage on any further CHA checks with that investor.

### Patronage

A patron is a form of investor, in that they help fund new businesses—from construction costs to starting inventory—with the bonus that they are often unconcerned with whether the business ever becomes profitable. They have their own reasons for wanting the business to exist, and their motives run the gamut from altruistic to unrealistic to outright diabolical. The GM can make the patron’s interests an ongoing mystery in the campaign, with the PCs constantly looking for loopholes in their agreement with the patron or wondering when the other shoe is going to drop.

### Guild Investors

When a guild invests in a new business, the guild masters expect the venture to turn a monthly profit of around 10% of the initial stake. Some guilds are much less forgiving than others of a merchant who fails to deliver on time.

However, because of their larger resources, a guild is also much more likely to provide more funds than an individual investor, including funds to build a new shop or workshop. However, the guild will enforce a long list of guild rules for the merchant to follow, and deviation from those rules requires persuasive arguments, if the shopkeeper hopes to keep the keys to their shop.

Guild rules can form the basis for an ongoing side quest to fulfill the guild’s requirements for membership, with the PC expected to succeed at an INT (History) check before they’re allowed to even open their doors to the public. The GM should set a DC commensurate with the nature of the guild’s business: that is, low for a guild that isn’t particularly powerful (such as a Bakers’ Guild), and much higher for a guild with a lot of clout (such as a Mason’s Guild or Goldsmith’s Guild). At the very least, the guild expects you to join and pay a membership fee. Under almost all circumstances, no guild will allow you to join if you are an active member of a different or rival guild.

Securing guild funding requires a successful CHA (Persuasion) check to convince the guild leaders to back your new endeavor. You can choose to use CHA (Deception) instead, but again, failure invokes their wrath, and you suffer disadvantage on any further CHA checks with the guild’s leaders until you set things right.

### Stepping Into a Business

In some cases, an established merchant seeks out someone to run an existing business. The physical shop is already there, the inventory is on the shelves, and even the customers may already be ready to buy. The owner just needs someone to mind the store.

If the GM presents the PCs with this sort of situation— perhaps as a reward for completing a quest—the PCs must make a CHA (Persuasion) check. The DC is for this check is determined based on the current Stake, which represents the value of the goods and the property. A success on this roll means that the PC has convinced the owner that they can be trusted to turn a profit in the owner’s absence.

**Management Difficulty**

| Managing Existing Stake | DC |
|---|---|
| 50 gp or less | 5 |
| 51−99 gp | 8 |
| 100−200 gp | 10 |
| 201−250 gp | 12 |
| 251−499 gp | 15 |
| 500−999 gp | 18 |
| 1,000−1,500 gp | 20 |
| 1,501−2,500 gp | 25 |
| 2,501−5,000 gp | 30 |

From that point forward, the owner will pay a visit to the PC at the end of each year to take a tally of the profits and losses. If things are going poorly, the PC should make another CHA (Persuasion) check to convince the owner not to immediately reclaim their property.

On the other hand, if the shop has done well, the owner rewards the PC with 20% of the profits. The PC can also make their case for a high percentage by rolling a DC 15 CHA (Persuasion) check. For every 2 points by which the PC’s roll exceeds the DC, they can claim an additional 5% of the profits.

## Components

If you are building your business from the ground up, consider how much your stock (and your customers) need to be protected from the elements—natural and criminal— and how you’re going to display your merchandise.

All of these have varying effects on your Stake and your daily commerce, per the rules in [[Running a Business]].

***Flooring.*** A basic building has a dirt floor, covered with straw to soak up spills, but they’re susceptible to pests. Wooden floorboards keep out pests, tile floors last longer, and stone floors are the best of all worlds in terms of what your customers stand on.

***Roofing.*** Ranging from straw thatch to slate shingles, roofing helps protect your shop from the elements.

***Walls.*** Basic walls are made of bundled straw, with wattle-and-daub walls being only a slight step up. Both are drafty and don’t keep moisture out particularly well. Cob, a kind of packed-earth mix, is sturdier, but requires more time to build, as well as a solid foundation. A properly built log structure is sturdy, quick, and keeps out most of the elements, but the best walls are made of brick, quicklime, or stone.

***Windows.*** Shutter windows let in both light and fresh air, or neither. Lead-glass windows allow light in, even when they’re closed.

***Doors.*** All doors provide some privacy, and maybe a little shelter from the elements, but the best doors also provide security.

***Locks.*** Common locks require either the key, or a successful DC 15 DEX (Thieves’ Tools) check. Magical locks require a key, the proper password, or a successful DC 25 DEX (Thieves’ Tools) check.

***Counter.*** This provides space for the merchant to display wares, examine loot, count coins, and conduct business.

***Workbench.*** A place for you to make things or repair them.

***Shelving.*** Shelving includes standard flat shelves as well as purpose-built units (for products that come in bottles, kegs, and so on). Having shelves lets you stock more items, thus increasing your shop’s Stake. (See [[Running a Business]].)

***Racks.*** Racks include tool and weapon racks, clothing racks, and so on. Having stock hanging from racks also increases your shop’s Stake.

***Signage.*** A sign lets passers-by know what kind of business you operate. A lack of signage means you suffer −2 to your Commerce Resolution checks.

***Awning.*** An awning means that your customers don’t have to stand in the rain or in the sun while they wait to get into your shop.

***Heating.*** A fireplace, or braziers, keep customers comfortable in inclement weather.

***Lighting.*** If your customers can’t see your wares, they can’t shop. Lighting varies in brightness and duration, with magical lighting being permanent.

***Display Cases.*** Display cases show off your wares and can be locked to prevent shoplifting. They also increase your Stake by giving you extra room to present your stock.

***Decorations.*** Decorations show potential customers that you want them to feel not just welcome but enriched when they visit your shop.

***Merchant’s Cage.*** A cage is a must for those merchants who need a little extra protection from robbers.

***Alarms.*** Mechanical alarms require a DC 15 DEX (Thieves’ Tools) check to disarm. Magical alarms require magic to disable.

***Lockboxes.*** A small lockbox keeps your day’s profits secure, requiring the key or a successful DC 15 (Thieves’ Tools) check to open. A standard metal-clad lockbox has AC 19 and 10 HP. It weighs 3 pounds.

***Safes.*** A safe keeps your total profits secure, requiring the key, or a successful DC 20 (Thieves’ Tools) check to open. A standard metal safe has AC 19 and 18 hp. Most weigh over 100 pounds.

***Strongbox.*** A strongbox is larger than a lockbox and not as heavy as a safe. It requires a key or a successful DC 18 (Thieves’ Tools) check to open. A standard oak-and-iron strongbox has AC 19 and 15 hp, and it weighs 15 pounds, making it at least somewhat lighter than a safe for a travelling merchant.

***Private Room.*** A private room inside a shop gives a merchant a place to hold a private conversation, negotiate large sales, or just take a break. It gives you a +1 bonus on CHA-based skill checks, because you can use the space to make a customer or supplier feel more comfortable…or just the opposite, if you’re employing Intimidation.

A private room can take up as much as half of the floorplan, but doing so cuts the shop’s Stake Limit in half, as well.

***Hidden Room.*** A private room with a concealed entrance. This can be a chamber for storing contraband, or a hidden hatch in the floor that leads to a small cellar, or something similar. A hidden room gives you a +2 bonus on CHA-based skill checks, because you can make private sales and hide any stock that you don’t want to become public knowledge.

A hidden room can take up as much as half of the floor plan but doing so cuts the shop’s Stake Limit in half, as well.

## Location

Where you set up shop can determine whether your business turns a profit. The farther you are from your customers (or the harder it is for them to reach your shop), the less likely you are to do much business each day. This generates a modifier to the check you make under the rules for **Running a Business*****.***

**Commerce Cost**

| Neighborhood | Land Cost/Rent Modifier | Commerce Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| Public Square | +10% | +50% |
| Market District | +5% | +25% |
| Industrial Center | — | — |
| Out-of-the-Way | −10% | −5% |
| Remote | −25% | −10% |

***Public Square.*** The public square is an ideal location for a shop, because people visit it daily.

***Market District.*** The market district is an obvious location for a business but isn’t as much a hub for public activity.

***Industrial Center.*** The default location for a shop is the neighborhood where the goods are manufactured or processed. (In smaller settlements, this might be identical to the Market District, or even the Public Square.)

***Out-of-the-Way.*** When customers must walk very far to visit your shop (including walking up several flights of stairs) or spend time searching for it, they’re more likely to give up and shop elsewhere.

***Remote.*** If visiting your shop is a major quest unto itself, only the most determined customers will come calling.

### Other Considerations

Sometimes, the success of a business hinges on something other than what the shop sells, or where it’s located. This section discusses the basic physical and social aspect of commerce, and how that affects each day’s custom.

***Demising.*** You can cut your startup costs in half by sharing your shop space with another business. However, this limits your display space, and the maximum result on your daily Commerce Resolution checks is 21−25 (20% of your Stake for the day). If the other business is complementary to yours (such as a café sharing a space with a bakery), you gain advantage on your rolls. If it is directly antithetical (such as a bakery sharing a space with a tanner), you suffer disadvantage.

***Anchor.*** Some shops are profitable only because something else in the vicinity drives business: a busy public area, a major industry, a religious center, or even another business. If this goes away, commerce dies off. While the anchor remains, you have advantage on your Commerce Resolution rolls. If it goes away, you have disadvantage.

***Traffic.*** The passers-by who might stop in is assumed in the cost of shop’s location or its rent. Low-traffic areas reduce the daily Commerce Resolution percentage; high- traffic areas increase it.

**Commerce Land Cost/Rent**

| Traffic | Land Cost/Rent Modifier | Commerce Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| Deserted | −20% | −10% |
| Slow | −10% | −5% |
| Average | — | — |
| Active | +2% | +5% |
| Bustling | +5% | +10% |
| Crowded | +8% | +25% |

***Reputation.*** A reputable shopkeeper brings customers in, and a poor reputation keeps them away. If you are proficient with Persuasion add your CHA modifier to your daily Commerce Resolution percentage. If you are proficient with either Deception or Intimidation, subtract your CHA modifier. (Note that these might cancel each other out, if a character is proficient in both Persuasion and one of the other two skills.)

***Frontage.*** How much of the building’s façade is devoted to the business affects how noticeable it is to passers-by. Smaller frontages reduce the daily Commerce Resolution percentage; larger ones increase it.

**Frontage Commerce Resolution Modifiers**

| Frontage | Land Cost/Rent Modifier | Commerce Modifier |
|---|---|---|
| Hidden | −10% | −5% |
| Small | −5% | −1% |
| Average | — | — |
| Obvious | +5% | +10% |
| Spectacular | +10% | +25% |

***Sanitation.*** If the settlement doesn’t have adequate sanitation (or the people to maintain it), garbage piles up, pests settle in, and foul odors drive away customers. If the shop’s location cannot dispose of waste (including byproducts of the shop’s crafting), you suffer disadvantage on your daily Commerce Resolution checks.

***Noise.*** External noises can make customers want to leave your shop quickly. If you don’t take steps to reduce the noise—such as covering windows with thick curtains, or magically negating or masking the sound— you suffer disadvantage on your daily Commerce Resolution checks.

***Safety.*** Setting up in a sketchy area means that upstanding citizens shop somewhere else. If your wares aren’t specifically meant to be sold to the criminal element in the neighborhood, you suffer disadvantage on your daily Commerce Resolution checks.

> [!note] Stake Limit by Shop Size
> Although a pound of diamonds is obviously worth more than a pound of flour, the Stake Limits listed in this section assume goods that sell for about 1 gp per pound. If a PC wishes to deal in goods that are worth more or less than that rough equivalent, multiply or divide the Stake Limit by up to 100.

## Mobile Shops

Sometimes you go where the business is. Sometimes, you can’t find an empty space to set up shop. Sometimes, your stock is so small that it’s not worth putting up a storefront. For situations like these, you need a storefront that you can set up quickly, take down quickly, and transport somewhere else, often at a moment’s notice.

Each of the options listed here has a size limitation that applies to your Stake. That is, the less inventory you can carry or haul, the less money you can make from selling it.

| Tiny Boat | 10 gp | 20 gp | — |
| Small Boat | 50 gp | 100 gp | 3 |
| Medium Boat | 150 gp | 250 gp | 9 |
| Large Boat | 250 gp | 500 gp | 20 |

***Peddler’s Pack.*** This pack is a cabinet full of the merchant’s wares, fitted with straps and carried on the merchant’s back. If the pack includes tools of the merchant’s trade (such as artist tools, clothier tools, and so on—for use in repairing items that the merchant sells), adjust the price in accordance with the **Tools** rules in **Chapter 5** of *Player’s Guide*.

If your shop is a peddler’s pack, your Frontage is considered Hidden, and the maximum result on your daily Commerce Resolution checks is 6−15 (a break-even result), although your total percentage can increase due to Traffic and Reputation. (See **Other Considerations**.)

***Peddler Cart.*** A slightly larger version of the peddler’s pack, this cart is best described as a cabinet on wheels. Its main advantage over the pack is that it makes transporting heavier goods easier.

If your shop is a peddler’s cart, your Frontage is considered Small, and the maximum result on your daily Commerce Resolution checks is 6−15 (a break-even result), although your total percentage can increase due to Traffic and Reputation. (See **Other Considerations**.)

***Donkey Cart.*** Although a beast of burden has obvious costs in care, fodder, and stabling, a cart can carry more goods than when a Humanoid is doing all the heavy lifting.

If your shop is a donkey cart, your Frontage is considered Small, and the maximum result on your daily Commerce Resolution checks is 16−20 (10% of your Stake for the day), although your total percentage can increase due to Traffic and Reputation. (See **Other Considerations**.)

***Wagon.*** A wagonload of goods can be eye-catching, and a merchant with a wagon can bring in more than the cost of feeding the draft horses or oxen that pull it.

If your shop is a wagon, your Frontage is considered Average, and the maximum result on your daily Commerce Resolution checks is 16−20 (10% of your Stake for the day), although your total percentage can increase due to Traffic and Reputation. (See **Other Considerations**.)

***Stall.*** The mainstay of marketplace merchants everywhere, a stall is a table with your goods laid out on or in front of it. While it’s not terribly secure, it doesn’t need to be, because the merchants watch over the stall and goods during the day. At night, you pack it up and take it home with you.

If your shop is a stall, your Frontage is considered Average, and the maximum result on your daily Commerce Resolution checks is 16−20 (10% of your Stake for the day), although your total percentage can increase due to Traffic and Reputation. (See **Other Considerations**.)

***Covered Stall.*** If your goods (or your customers) don’t react well to the elements, you can provide them with an awning to stand under while they peruse your wares. A covered stall adds +1 to your Commerce Resolution roll (up to the 10% limit) but is otherwise functionally identical to a stall.

***Tent.*** A merchant’s tent provides not just shelter from the elements and a bit of shade to keep customers comfortable, but it also creates a bit of a private space, which other portable shops rarely manage.

If your shop is a tent, add +3 to your Commerce Resolution roll, and your Frontage is considered Obvious. The maximum result on your daily Commerce Resolution checks is 16−20 (10% of your Stake for the day), although your total percentage can increase due to Traffic and Reputation. (See **Other Considerations**.)

***Pavilion.*** A larger, grander version of the merchant’s tent, a pavilion provides the same essential benefits, but you can stock more items, and can even have screened areas inside to give customers a little privacy.

If your shop is a pavilion, add +3 to your Commerce Resolution roll, your Frontage is considered Obvious, and the maximum result on your daily Commerce Resolution checks is 21−25 (20% of your Stake for the day), although your total percentage can increase due to Traffic and Reputation. (See **Other Considerations**.)

***Tiny Boat.*** This is a small rowboat or coracle for casting a fishing net (or diving for clams and so on). At about 40 square feet, it is suitable for lakes and more placid rivers but is not at all seaworthy.

Because you sell your catch directly from your boat—almost always located on the shore—you can’t really take advantage of the Public Square (see **Location**), and the maximum Stake is very limited.

***Small Boat.*** A slightly larger watercraft at 80 square feet, boats of this size have both fishing nets and a small hold to store your catch and supplies. (You can even sleep in it, if necessary.) It has a small sail, but can also be rowed in a pinch, and it can safely operate on lakes, rivers, and the calmer seas, though it frequently founders in rough water.

You are still limited to selling from the shore or the docks (so you can’t take advantage of the traffic that passes through the Public Square; see **Location**).

***Medium Boat.*** A common vessel of this size might be a ketch or a dogger, and it measures 240 square feet. It includes multiple fishing nets or crab pots, with a cramped area for the crew and cargo below deck. A medium boat is mostly wind-propelled, via a mainsail and mizzen sail, and possibly a foresail. It’s too large for smaller lakes and rivers, but it handles well on open water and is considerably more seaworthy; it can make journeys of a few weeks, if adequately provisioned. You are still limited to selling from the docks or just offshore.

***Large Boat.*** Cogs are the most common fishing or trading vessel at this size, measuring roughly 1,200 square feet, with three sails, a below-deck area for the crew, and a much larger below-deck area for cargo. Too large for most inland waterways, it shines on prolonged sea voyages. You are still limited to selling from the docks or just offshore.

## Permanent Shops

The best shortcut to setting up a business is to buy someone else’s business—or at least the building it occupied—and set up shop there. This is also a quick and easy way to establish a basic floor plan, which you can either use as-is, or modify using the rules listed under **Component Descriptions**.

As with mobile shops, each of the options listed here has a size limitation that applies to your Stake.

**Permanent Shops**

| Item | Cost | Stake Limit | Hireling Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tiny Shop | 100 gp | 200 gp | — |
| Small Shop | 250 gp | 400 gp | 1 |
| Medium Shop | 500 gp | 800 gp | 3 |
| Large Shop | 1,000 gp | 1,500 gp | 7 |
| Shop w/Apartment | 1,000 gp | 1,500 gp | 5 |
| Shop w/House | 2,500 gp | 5,000 gp | 7* |
| House w/Shop | 2,000 gp | 4,000 gp | 4** |

\* The house portion has living space for four people. ** The house portion has living space for eight people.

***Tiny Shop.*** This shop is a 100-square-foot space, with wood flooring, a wood shingle roof, four wooden or cob walls, one wooden door, a single shuttered window, and no locks. A small table acts as a counter, with a single hanging lamp for lighting and a signpost outside to advertise the business.

***Small Shop.*** This shop is 400 square feet, with wood flooring, a wood shingle roof, four wooden or cob walls, two wooden doors with standard locks, and four shuttered windows. It comes equipped with a cabinet-style counter with a built-in display case, four 5-foot shelves, four hanging lamps, and a signpost.

***Medium Shop.*** This shop measures 900 square feet, often including a second floor or loft (or cellar) which can double as a private area (or a hidden room). The flooring and roofing are wooden, the walls are made of cob or wood, as are the three doors (one of which may lead to the second floor/loft or cellar). Each door has a standard lock built in.

***Large Shop.*** The largest of shops measures a total of 1,600 square feet, and almost certainly occupies more than one floor. As with other sizes of shops, the flooring, roofing, and doors are made of wood, and the walls are either wood or cob. It has a total of four doors, including at least one that leads to a second floor or cellar, and all of which are equipped with standard locks.

***Shop with Attached Apartment.*** Many shops are part of the merchant’s residence, with a lockable door between the public and private portions of the structure. This ensures that the merchant is never too far from a customer with money to spend, and it doesn’t hurt that they aren’t paying two separate rents.

A shop of this sort is essentially a Large Shop, with some of the space set aside as a Private Room. However, the attached apartment reduces the number of additional workers from seven down to five (even if you give them run of the apartment).

***Shop with Attached House.*** The luxury version of the attached apartment is the attached house. This, too, is comparable to a Large Shop in terms of its components, but the house is its own entity, rather than just being a small room with a bed.

Note that up to 25% of the shop’s inventory can be stored in the house portion of this structure, but if so, it does not count toward the shop’s Stake, for purposes of **Commerce Resolution** rolls.

***House with Attached Shop.*** Exactly the opposite of the attached-house version of this shop, this structure prioritizes the house space over the shop space.

Note that up to 75% of the shop’s inventory can be stored in the house portion of this structure, but if so, it does not count toward the shop’s Stake, for purposes of **Commerce Resolution** rolls.

## Renting an Existing Shop

Although this section deals with purchasing buildings (and the parcels of land under them), the traditional method of starting up a new shop is to rent an existing one, preferably one that is already set up for the kind of business you’re planning.

Renting an existing shop costs only 10% of the prices listed in the **Location**, **Mobile Shops**, **Permanent Shops**, and **Workshops, Factories, and Farms** sections. Rent is paid annually, whether you use the shop or not. For enterprising PCs on a budget, this is the most affordable approach, but it comes with two significant downsides.

### Customization Limits

When searching for just the right shop to rent, the PC can choose items from the **Components** list to adjust to their specifications by simply paying the listed price: swapping out dirt flooring for tile flooring, for example, or adding an awning or a decorative fountain. The PC can also downgrade these components—swapping out tile flooring for dirt flooring, for example—to reduce the total price of the property, and therefore, reduce the rent.

While selecting the specific building where the PC wants to set up shop, the player can make a maximum of three such changes, in these Component categories: Flooring, Roofing, Walls, Doors, and Windows, plus Heating. This represents the PC shopping for an overall structure that suits their needs, without letting the player custom-design their new shop but only pay rental pricing for it.

Note that after the PC has paid their annual rent on the property, they are free to make whatever changes they like; the building is effectively theirs until rent is due again. There are two rules to consider, however:

- *The cost is paid by the PC*, and because it improves the value of the property, it adds to the price of the next year’s rent. Downgrading a given component, however (such as replacing one stone wall with a wooden wall, for example), does *not* lower the rent, and might break the Terms of the Lease.

- *The PC can make* temporary *changes without penalty*, such as adding or removing any Components that aren’t from the Flooring, Roofing, Walls, Doors, or Windows categories. That is, they are free to add decorative fountains, signage, hidden rooms, and so on, without their modifications impacting the rent.

### Terms of the Lease

The other significant downside to renting is that the PC has a contract with the landlord. Such a contract is pretty vague, though, and mostly stipulates that the landlord won’t bother the PC so long as the PC is a good and reputable tenant…but that any disputes about the landlord’s rights versus the tenant’s rights are up to a court to decide.

Some examples of rental conditions include:

- ***Damage to the Property:*** Any damage to the property—from unauthorized changes to the building (see **Customization Limits**) to a pitched battle on the premises—may be grounds for eviction. Thanks to chatty neighbors who know the landlord, the property owner will visit the PCs shortly after any significant damage occurs, and the PCs must pay for repairs in a timely fashion. The PC may attempt a DC 15 CHA (Persuasion) check to buy a little grace, but if repairs are not concluded (or reimbursed to the landlord) within a month, the PCs will be promptly evicted.

- ***Nuisance to the Neighborhood:*** Activities that endanger the neighbors or their livelihoods—or are just annoying—also prompt a visit from the landlord, who warn the PCs to correct the issue immediately, then return after a short time to check that the nuisance has been addressed. Again, the PC may attempt a DC 15 CHA (Persuasion) check to soften the landlord’s attitude, but ultimately, the neighbors need to be convinced to ignore the problem. If the nuisance continues, the landlord can evict the tenant.

- ***Notice of Rent Increase:*** Although it can be a tactic to force a tenant out, the landlord may raise the rent, mid-term, by any amount they wish…but the landlord is usually required to give a week’s notice. At the end of that week, the PC must pay the difference in rent (prorated based on how much time was left on the old lease) or pack up and leave.

- ***Eviction Notice:*** Even when the PCs can convince the owner to give them more time to address an issue, such things add up, and the owner may eventually decide to throw the PC out, along with all of their goods and personal belongings. In general, the tenant has one day to clear out, but succeeding at a DC 15 CHA (Persuasion) check can extend that notice to three days. However, short of a particularly convincing argument (or a judicious application of enchantment magic), the eviction is unavoidable.
