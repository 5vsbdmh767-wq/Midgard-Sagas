*Optional, from Trade Secrets.*

In roleplaying games and in storytelling, creating a business is much like building a character. Each business is brought to life by both broad strokes and minute details that make it unique and interesting. This chapter outlines and walks through the creative process of defining a business and bringing it to life in gameplay. For the rules on earning money from a business, see [[Running a Business]].

## Five Steps for Success

This section covers five steps for creating a fun and unique business for your campaign.

### Step 1: Choose a Location

A supplies shop in an evil wizard’s fortress is markedly different from one in a bucolic village. Location determines what customers the business has, what those customers need, what rules must be followed, what goods can be sourced, security needs, and many other concerns. Most merchants do business in the place they live in and know best, both out of convenience and because their local knowledge is a big advantage over outside competitors. Of course, some businesses are about being mobile or serving as a bridge between different communities, but they can’t be everywhere all the time, so defining where they trade is still a vital decision.

### Step 2: Choose a Trade

The business must offer something in demand that the business can supply. The ideal position is to be the only business providing a given good or service, but most businesses face competition of some kind. The kind of trade a business conducts defines many other aspects, such as its customers, its employees, and its stock of goods.

### Step 3: Acquire Inventory

A business sells goods, uses capital, must have access to labor, and needs supplies on hand to run smoothly. Whether you’re dealing with a PC or NPC enterprise determines the details, but either way, it’s a critical area for defining a mercantile business.

### Step 4: Make a Merchant

Every business needs a merchant who runs the day-to-day operations. That manager may also be the owner, but not everyone with gold to invest has the knowledge to run the show. Adventurer-owned businesses need someone to dedicate time to the business while their boss is away plundering ancient tombs. Innkeepers, shopkeepers, guild leaders, and others function as the face of a given business and the person whom the PCs interact with when visiting it.

### Step 5: Choose a Name and Details

Every business needs a memorable and evocative name. This not only helps it market its goods and services but also goes a long way to expressing its unique character and style. To make a fantasy business stand out, it also needs the little quirks and specifics that make it distinct from others in the same trade. These details bring the business to life during play.

## Choose a Location

A business’s location is essential to its success and often determines vital details about its operations. While this section is about choosing a location, you could also start with a location and consider what businesses could operate there. Characters usually establish a business wherever they call home, while GMs detail businesses in whatever places their campaigns take the PCs.

### Businesses by Settlement Size

At first blush, running a business where the most potential customers live seems like a clever idea, but competition and other considerations can change that calculation—it’s better to be the only seller in a small town than one of a dozen in a major city. Ultimately, the right place for a given business (or the right business for a given place) depends on knowing where goods are needed most and where buyers might be willing to pay for the right shop to solve a problem.

### Settlements and Hamlets

While the pool of customers in a small community is limited, the lack of competition is a big advantage for a thriving business there. This often means a business gains an effective monopoly, but gouging the locals can lead to deep anger and resentment. Typically, the merchants in small towns form a close relationship and a sense of shared prosperity with their customers.

Small communities are a poor choice for highly specialized businesses. Instead, a successful small-town business offers a wide range of related products or services the town needs. Many small farming, mining, or timber communities, need businesses that buy, produce, or sell farming supplies, foodstuffs, ore, or mining and lumbering tools.

Traveling merchants serve multiple small communities by making reliable stops along a circuit. These traveling merchants gain all the benefits of being the only seller in town while still serving far more customers than any one village can generate.

### Villages and Towns

Villages and towns host a range of mercantile businesses, especially if they are easily accessible by road or port. They have enough customers to support specialized merchants year-round and a large-enough variety of commercial activity to demand specialized goods and services.

Such communities typically have a dominant local industry or trade based on the natural resources found nearby. Most trading businesses either focus on that industry or offer the kinds of general services every town needs, such as food and lodging. Still, middle-sized communities offer opportunities for more niche businesses if the merchant can market wares effectively.

### Cities and Metropolises

Large metropolitan cities are centers of trade for an entire region or nation. Most start with a local industry, but by virtue of their accessible location become a true crossroads of commerce. They become the center of financial, political, and military interest as the concentration of wealth and population grows. Banking, military, and artistic pursuits are all viable businesses in a large city.

For any merchant, the sky is the limit for potential success and for the types of business that can thrive in such a place. A metropolis can support the most specialized businesses thanks to the incredible variety of customers from every trade, income level, and walk of life. Competition becomes a key consideration for a business in this environment. Finding a unique niche can be a significant challenge.

Most cities of this size have distinct neighborhoods or districts that are effectively medium-sized towns by themselves. These vary based on the wealth of the residents, the natural geography, and the principle commercial activities concentrated there. While trade happens in every district, dedicated shopping and service districts make it easy for merchants to buy and sell in volume; they simplify warehousing near a market or bazaar for specialized or perishable goods.

### Building a Shopping District

A key market district within a metropolis makes an excellent stage for mercantile adventures and intrigue. Consider key elements such as the district governor, the guard captain, known criminal gangs, and the most prosperous businesses in the district. The trade goods the district is known for might be anything: a fish market, a clothing market, a horse fair, or straightforward food and drink—or it might be services, such as a finance and accounting district run by keen-eyed elves or a dragonborn’s playhouse surrounded by bars and restaurants.

### Businesses in Unusual Environments

Commerce knows no true boundaries and where there are people to trade with, there is trade. From the depths of the ocean to the darkness of the underworld and even into other planes of reality, merchant enterprises abound. The types of goods and services often are bizarre to outsiders, but like any trade, they are dictated by the needs of their customers and the circumstances of the environment. Think big: scented air bubbles and underwater mounts, mushroom textiles, demonic metals, glass weapons, enchanted pets, or trade in ghosts and shades.

## Choose a Trade

Every business provides goods or services that solve a problem or satisfy a need other people have. The possibilities are nearly endless, but the following are some of the common categories for trade.

### Primary Industries

Primary industries are businesses that form the economic foundation of settlements and communities. This category includes both the collection of important raw materials and the production of essential foodstuffs. This type of work often involves hard manual labor as well as control of land that can yield the resources.

#### Agriculture

Societies that subsist primarily by domesticating plants and animals also develop higher levels of labor specialization, which naturally leads to more complex patterns of commerce, which in turn gives rise to mercantile professions.

##### Growing Crops

Using the land to grow crops is one of the oldest industries and is a mainstay of civilization. Food crops are the most common, but many useful resources can be cultivated on a farm, including fiber, medicine, and fuel.

***Capital.*** Irrigation, fertile land, draft animals, farming implements

***Labor.*** Farm hands

***Supplies.*** Seedstock

***Goods.*** Grain, vegetables, fiber, oils, medicine, fuel, dyes

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Nature

***Risks.*** Weather variations, crop diseases, pests

##### Raising Livestock

Animal husbandry is another ancient practice, one at least as old as cultivation.

***Capital.*** Pasture

***Labor.*** Farm hands, shepherds

***Supplies.*** Breeding stock, feed

***Goods.*** Meat, hides, fiber, bone, dairy, eggs, fuel, medicine

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Medicine, Nature

***Risks.*** Disease, predators, theft

#### Foraging

Foraging involves collecting plants and animals from the wilderness. It’s one of the oldest and most fundamental types of work and enterprise. While foraging can benefit from various capital investments, it rarely requires any inputs beyond those needed by the people doing the work.

##### Fishing

Fishing is a critical food source for coastal and river regions and can scale up far more than hunting, especially in coastal regions.

***Capital.*** Boats, nets, traps, fishing gear

***Labor.*** Anglers, fishers

***Supplies.*** None

***Goods.*** Meat, oils, shells, medicine, pearls

***Proficiencies.*** Athletics, Nature, Stealth, Survival

***Risks.*** Drowning, storms, overharvesting

##### Forestry

While forests provide numerous resources, timber is the most important. It is key to making ships, buildings, tools, and more, but its most critical use is for the fuel that makes other industries possible. While incredibly abundant in many places, it’s slow to replenish once depleted.

***Capital.*** Forest lands, axes, saws, draft animals

***Labor.*** Lumberjacks, porters

***Supplies.*** None

***Goods.*** Lumber, fiber, fuel

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Athletics, Nature, Survival

***Risks.*** Accidents, overharvesting

##### Hunting and Trapping

Wild animals can provide a wide range of resources, much like animal husbandry can, but with few inputs beyond labor and tools.

***Capital.*** Game lands, weapons, trapper tools, hunting animals

***Labor.*** Hunters

***Supplies.*** None

***Goods.*** Meat, hides, fiber, bone, horn, fuel, medicine

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Athletics, Perception,

Nature, Stealth, Survival, trapper tools

***Risks.*** Accidents, animal attacks, overharvesting

##### Monster Hunting

Monster hunting is more dangerous and difficult than hunting common animals. Not only are most monsters aggressive killers, but they are also more difficult to find than deer or wolves. All this makes the products of a monster hunt more valuable than simple pelts, bone, and meat.

***Capital.*** Weapons, armor, trapper tools, wildlands, magical equipment

***Labor.*** Adventurers, mercenaries, hunters

***Supplies.*** None

***Goods.*** Meat, hides, fiber, bone, horn, medicine, magical components

***Proficiencies.*** Arcana, Athletics, Perception, Religion,

Stealth, Survival

***Risks.*** Dismemberment, death, curses, annihilation of the soul

#### Mining

Mining involves collecting mineral resources in massive quantities, sometimes from the surface, and sometimes by digging deep into earth and stone. It can be exceptionally dangerous and demanding work. It’s like foraging except minerals don’t replenish over time and eventually become depleted in a given location.

***Capital.*** Mineral deposits, mine shafts, draft animals, mining equipment

***Labor.*** Laborers, miners

***Supplies.*** None

***Goods.*** Stone, salt, ore, gems, crystals, clay

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Athletics, Investigation,

Nature

***Risks.*** Cave-in, landslides, flooding, suffocation, poisoning

### Secondary Industries

Secondary industries take the raw materials from primary industries and turn them into either finished goods for consumption or into inputs for other secondary industries.

#### Construction

Construction can be as simple as a mud hut or as complex as a grand cathedral. As the complexity of a project rises, the number of specialized craftspeople also rises.

***Capital.*** Land, cranes, construction tools, draft animals

***Labor.*** Architects, carpenters, engineers, laborers, masons

***Supplies.*** Stone, timber, clay, gravel, glass, fixtures, furniture, fiber

***Goods.*** Buildings, bridges, roads, docks, fortifications

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Athletics, construction tools, Investigation

***Risks.*** Accidents, collapse, earthquakes, storms

#### Consumer Goods

Consumer goods has a specific meaning: finished goods, tools, and materials sold to the public. It does not include raw materials or unworked goods, except for home crafts such as weaving or cooking.

***Capital.*** Workshops, crafting tools, artist tools

***Labor.*** Craftspeople, laborers

***Supplies.*** Wood, fiber, metal, leather, horn, bone, dyes

***Goods.*** Various consumer goods

***Proficiencies.*** Artist tools

***Risks.*** None

#### Siege Weapons

Creating siege engines is a specialized industry that primarily serves armies with enough forces to initiate and maintain a siege.

***Capital.*** Workshop, construction tools, tinker tools

***Labor.*** Engineers, carpenters, blacksmiths, laborers

***Supplies.*** Wood, iron

***Goods.*** Siege Weapons

***Proficiencies.*** Construction tools, tinker tools

***Risks.*** Accidents, politics

#### Vehicles

While wagons and carts are important tools for transportation on land, their importance pales in comparison to shipbuilding. Ships are essential for both fishing and for moving people and commodities on a scale and at a cost that is impossible using overland routes.

***Capital.*** Ports, workshops, construction tools

***Labor.*** Engineers, shipwrights, carpenters, blacksmiths, laborers

***Supplies.*** Timber, fiber, fuel, pitch, iron, cloth

***Goods.*** Carts, ships, wagons

***Proficiencies.*** Construction tools

***Risks.*** Accidents, storms

#### Cooking and Brewing

Creating food and drink is one of the great arts of civilization and remains a crucial commercial activity. It’s both necessary for survival and one of life’s great luxuries.

***Capital.*** Hearth, provisioner tools

***Labor.*** Cooks

***Supplies.*** Fuel, grain, meat, spices, vegetables, water

***Goods.*** Drink, foodstuffs

***Proficiencies.*** Provisioner tools

***Risks.*** Allergies, disease, poison

#### Handcrafts

Handcrafts include a range of individually produced artistic objects. Some handcrafts are made to be works of art, such as paintings and sculptures, while others are decorative and beautified versions of household items.

***Capital.*** Workshop, artist tools

***Labor.*** Artists, craftspeople

***Supplies.*** Clay, cloth, dye, fiber, leather, metals, stone, wood

***Goods.*** Jewelry, painting, pottery, sculpture

***Proficiencies.*** Artist tools

***Risks.*** None

#### Metalworking

Metalworking creates a wide range of tools that enable other crafts and enhances the collection of raw materials. Metalworking is also a key input for hunting and warfare.

***Capital.*** Forge, smithing tools

***Labor.*** Blacksmiths

***Supplies.*** Fuel, ore

***Goods.*** Armor, construction supplies, tools, weapons

***Proficiencies.*** Smithing tools

***Risks.*** Accidents, burns

#### Textiles

Textile workers turn fiber into thread, thread into cloth, and cloth into finished clothing, armor, tapestries, rugs, and many other products.

***Capital.*** Clothier tools, loom

***Labor.*** Fashion designers, knitters, seamsters, spinners, weavers

***Supplies.*** Fiber, dye

***Goods.*** Cloth, clothing, rugs, tapestries, thread

***Proficiencies.*** Clothier tools

***Risks.*** None

### Services

Labor exists at the heart of every industry. Where services differ from others is the lack of a physical product at the end of the process.

#### Sales

Sales work is about matchmaking between goods and customers, getting things people need into the hands of the people who need them.

##### Retail

Retailers sell consumer goods, usually in a storefront, market stall, or as a traveling salesperson. Retailers find products that customers need and convince customers they need the products the retailer has on hand.

***Capital.*** Storefront

***Labor.*** Salespeople, security

***Goods.*** Consumer goods

***Service.*** Customer service

***Proficiencies.*** Insight, Persuasion

***Risks.*** Demand and supply shifts

##### Wholesale

Wholesalers bridge the gaps between producers and retailers, typically when the scale of operations differs significantly. Producers typically make far more than any one retailer can sell, and most retailers sell a wider range of products than any one producer makes. Wholesalers also provide warehousing so the temporal gap between production and sale can be evened out and managed.

***Capital.*** Warehouses

***Labor.*** Salespeople, security

***Goods.*** Any trade good

***Service.*** Finding large-scale sellers for goods and finding goods for other businesses

***Proficiencies.*** Insight, Persuasion

***Risks.*** Demand and supply shifts

### Transportation and Communications

Valuable but scarce goods easily justify the cost of transporting them from a place where the cost to produce them is far lower than their potential value. Carrying messages transports communications written on scrolls, tablets, or foolscap parchment.

#### Moving Goods

Moving goods becomes more complex over larger distances because the risks become both more numerous and more unpredictable. Even if you succeed in transporting something, the cost of doing so may vary wildly. This tends to make it a boom-or-bust business where the potential profits are huge, but so are the risks.

***Capital.*** Carts, draft animals, maps, ships, wagons

***Labor.*** Caravanners, laborers, mercenaries, navigators, sailors

***Supplies.*** Provisions

***Service.*** Transporting goods between markets

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Athletics, Nature,

Perception, Survival

***Risks.*** Accidents, monsters, robbery, weather

#### Moving Messages

While messengers and communication might not seem directly related to transport, traders, carters, ship captains, and others are the ones who speed communication between cities. In many realms, no postal service exists, only messengers and informal networks. Magical communications is also a specialized, paid service. The right message at the right time can move the world.

***Capital.*** Fast animals, maps, ships, sorting

***Labor.*** Clerks, couriers, delivery teams, runners, riders, stable keepers

***Supplies.*** Provisions

***Service.*** Transporting messages and parcels between markets

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Athletics, Nature,

Perception, Survival

***Risks.*** Accidents, monsters, robbery, weather

#### Moving People

Transporting people has advantages and disadvantages over goods. While people are usually self-reliant, they can also require additional care and support, including medicine and psychological needs. Wealthy passengers require the most accommodation but also pay far better than commoners do.

***Capital.*** Carts, draft animals, maps, ships, wagons

***Labor.*** Caravanners, mercenaries, navigators, sailors, stewards

***Supplies.*** Medicine, provisions

***Service.*** Transporting people between destinations

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling, Insight, Medicine, Nature,

Perception, Persuasion, Survival

***Risks.*** Accidents, monsters, robbery, weather

### Skilled Services

Some services depend on a skilled and educated practitioner, often of high status.

#### Education

Professional educators are something of a rare breed in places where the work consists of practical hands-on activities. Most of their work comes from tutoring children of wealthy or noble families. Apprenticeships allow the student to trade labor in exchange for learning under an experienced master.

***Capital.*** Schools

***Labor.*** Teachers

***Supplies.*** Books, manuscripts, teaching aids

***Services.*** Teaching lessons and writing instructions

***Proficiencies.*** Whatever proficiency is being taught

***Risks.*** None

#### Entertainment

Professional entertainers earn their living by making people feel good. People who can make a living entertaining are charismatic or talented. Because entertainment often benefits from novelty, many professional entertainers travel widely to find fresh audiences.

***Capital.*** Venues

***Labor.*** Entertainers, laborers

***Supplies.*** Costumes, makeup, instruments

***Services.*** Acting, playing instruments, singing, storytelling

***Proficiencies.*** Performance

***Risks.*** Hostile audiences

#### Hospitality

Hospitality services provide the comforts of home to travelers and luxuries to those who don’t enjoy them at home. Bars, restaurants, bath houses, inns, stables, and similar accommodations all aid in making an evening a little bit better.

***Capital.*** Buildings, furniture, provisioner tools

***Labor.*** Labor, cooks, servers

***Supplies.*** Fuel, provisions

***Proficiencies.*** Animal Handling,

Insight, provisioner tools

***Risks.*** Disease, fire

#### Finance

Financial institutions provide loans to businesses and governments. The flip side of providing loans is creating investment opportunities for anyone with enough idle currency to make the loans possible. Beyond these banking services, there are also professional accountants, tax collectors, currency traders, and others who work almost exclusively with currency. On the less savory side of town, loan sharks, bookies, and gambling houses also deal in the business of currency.

***Capital.*** Currency

***Labor.*** Accountants, bankers, security

***Supplies.*** Currency

***Proficiencies.*** Deception, Sleight of Hand, Intimidation,

Investigation, Persuasion

***Risks.*** Fraud, theft, unpaid debts

#### Medicine

There is always a demand for medical services. Powerful clerics and druids can bring life back to the dead, while mundane healers work in every community using minor magics and natural remedies for all manner of illnesses.

***Capital.*** Herbalist tools, holy symbols, magic items

***Labor.*** Clerics, bards, druids, herbalists, rangers, warlocks, wizards

***Supplies.*** Bandages, clean water, herbs, medicine, potions, spell components

***Proficiencies.*** Arcana, Herbalist tools, Medicine, Nature,

Religion

***Risks.*** Disease, poison, unnatural bargains

#### Personal Services

Busy, rich, and powerful people often hire others to handle many mundane tasks for themselves and their families. Butlers, gardeners, groomers, housekeepers, launderers, and the like all serve in this capacity. Specialists can perform house calls, while others might have a storefront, market stall, or home business serving dozens or hundreds of clients.

***Capital.*** Varies

***Labor.*** Varies

***Supplies.*** Varies

***Proficiencies.*** Varies

***Risks.*** None

#### Mercenaries

Most mercenaries are fighters of some kind, but mercenary companies may include officers, scouts, spellcasters, and spies. Adventurers can find employment as mercenaries during wartime, especially when the enemy includes monstrous or supernatural armies. Mercenaries rarely sign up for do-or-die missions and often act as reserve armies to free regular troops to deploy to the front lines.

***Capital.*** Armor, magic items, weapons

***Labor.*** Adventurers, soldiers, strategists

***Supplies.*** Provisions

***Proficiencies.*** Arcana, Athletics, Intimidation

***Risks.*** Violence

#### Security

For security professionals, violence is the last resort, not their primary occupation. The most common are armed guards, but in a world of magic, spellcasters work to provide magical protection against arcane threats. For those looking for the most versatile sort of security, hiring adventuring parties is often ideal.

***Capital.*** Armor, magic items, trapper tools, weapons

***Labor.*** Adventurers, guards, trap makers, spellcasters

***Supplies.*** Provisions, trap parts, spell components

***Proficiencies.*** Arcana, Intimidation, Insight, Perception,

Stealth

***Risks.*** Violence

## Acquire Inventory

A business can count four categories of assets as part of its overall inventory: goods, capital, labor, and supplies. Each business has a mix of these assets.

### Player-Owned Business

Players are interested in how much profit their business can generate, so they need to know how much they are paying for their capital, labor, and supplies, as well as the prices and quantity of goods they can stock and sell.

If both the GM and players are excited about keeping detailed records for a fantasy business, dive in and have fun. Not everyone wants to play with spreadsheets, though, so simplify where you can. Because the focus is usually on income, you can forgo a detailed inventory and instead focus on the bottom line for each category.

***Capital.*** For capital, the GM should establish an upfront cost. The player characters need to spend funds or acquire land, machines, and so forth to get their business started. This could involve taking on financial partners or loans, which can lead to conflict or intrigue—or it might be an investment from a prince, a wealthy wizard, or other patron who takes most of the profits.

***Goods and Services.*** Revenue for the shop is the result of how much the characters’ goods cost and how much they can sell over a thirty-day period. Players should decide what goods they want their shop to carry, and the GM should determine a baseline of sales in gold pieces for a typical period. From there, players can try to find ways to increase sales.

***Labor.*** Hiring can be a fun roleplaying scene for GMs who like to create interesting NPCs. These can then become story hooks for further adventures. If the business is kept in the background, then the only important thing about workers for the game is how much they are paid.

***Supplies.*** Players should understand how the business is getting the supplies needed to stay in business; perhaps the characters have a steady supplier, or perhaps the supplies are seasonal or intermittent due to shipping or the ebb and flow of magic. However, tracking them in detail is rarely interesting. The GM should determine a reasonable supply cost for each financial period. Securing or defending a supply source can be a good story hook for an adventure.

### Capital

Capital includes all the long-term or permanent assets a business needs to provide goods or services. The most basic capital is land, either from which to extract resources or to serve as a point of interaction with customers. Manufacturing businesses often have a set of tools, machinery, and a workshop that represent their primary capital investments: a smithy, for example, or a cooper’s shed. Beasts of burden or livestock animals also fall under capital, as they continually produce some good or service over time, even if they eventually stop being productive.

Detail a business’s capital assets with a simple list, keeping in mind what the operation requires on a repeated daily basis. Use real-world examples to help you come up with ideas. For example, an inn needs all the following to serve its patrons:

- A building with private rooms at a convenient site
- Furniture, such as beds, tables, desks, chairs, and armoires
- Linens, such as towels, washcloths, and bedsheets
- If the inn also serves meals, it needs a fully outfitted kitchen, plus stocks of food and drink, plates, cups, and utensils, and so on
- A well for drinking and bathing water
- A stable for housing mounts and beasts of burden
- Firewood to keep the building warm in cool months
- Lanterns or lamps plus oil, and candles

### Goods and Services

For businesses that specialize in one type of good or service, little other than price is worth worrying about. On the other hand, a pawn shop or a vendor buying or selling magical treasures is going to have a vast inventory. Trying to detail every item in a shop at a given moment is difficult enough, but trying to keep track of inventory over time can overwhelm even the most organized GM.

Fortunately, this level of record keeping is unnecessary. Any PC-run shops, just like a PC’s inventory, are for players to keep track of; the GM only checks in to make sure it’s in keeping with the campaign. For NPC shops, the GM simply can decide whether an item is in stock. If the PCs don’t know what they want, decide what items an NPC is most eager to sell them.

### Labor

Every business has at least one labor asset: its owner. This also often includes the owner’s family, and only occasionally does a business gain employees from outside the family. Certainly, occasional and part-time workers can be included in a business’s labor inventory.

In service-based businesses, labor is often the most important asset, as the skills of a trade make it possible to operate. Most NPC businesses revolve around their owner, an expert or long-time practitioner who represents the business to the PCs during the game. In PC-owned businesses, the NPC’s workers can become important side characters (or even covert antagonists, if they also work for a rival or steal from the business).

It’s certainly not worth developing game stats for every single worker in a large business, because few of them voluntarily join in combat. However, make notes about useful skills or interesting personality traits they may have.

### Supplies

Business supplies include any consumable good used to conduct business. Supplies in inventory are what the business needs to run and thus stockpiles in large quantities. Supplies are purchased from large businesses specializing in such materials (for example, a miller selling flour to a bakery); supplies like this are rarely bought in the market square. As a result of buying in bulk, businesses typically pay a lower price than an individual would.

#### Scarcity of Supplies

The availability of supplies can make or break a business. Without supplies, some businesses simply can’t operate, and if the cost of supplies outpaces the revenue from sales, it can’t operate profitably. In either case, a supply disruption makes a fine adventure hook.

## Choose a Name and Details

### Naming a Business

A business name can be incredibly important or largely meaningless. Typically, the more public facing the business is and the more competition it has, the more important a good name becomes.

#### Naming Conventions

There are three common naming conventions for businesses that feel appropriate for a fantasy setting.

***Descriptive Names.*** Descriptive names describe what the business does or why someone might want to use them. Examples might include “Great Value Grocery,” or “Reliable Potions.” Whether the claims are true, customers know what kind of business it is and have at least a little assurance they will be happy with their purchase.

***Iconography Names.*** When a significant portion of the population is illiterate, many public businesses use one or two images to represent their business name. For example, a stable might have a picture of a horse and an apple, so it would be known as “The Horse and Apple stables.” Using two icons tends to preclude any duplicate names, but a single, evocative icon could evoke something like “The Drunken Dwarf,” or “The Blushing Maid.”

***The Owner’s Name.*** Many businesses simply go by the name of their owner, especially small businesses where the owner is the primary craftsperson or salesperson. This has the advantage of imparting the owner’s reputation to the business. If they are a trusted person in the community, then customers trust the business. It’s also the name that people use when they don’t know what a business is called. “Oh, that’s Little Jim’s herb shop over there.” When a business is a partnership, it may bear the names of each partner such as, “Suzan and Katie’s Meat Pies.”

### Security

No business is entirely secure, but every business makes some effort to protect its property. In a world of fantasy, magic complicates things, both when securing property and when trying to steal it.

Some security measures are obvious to visitors, while others can be well hidden. Visible security measures discourage impulsive thefts. The hidden ones prevent more determined thieves from getting away with much.

#### Nonmagical Security

***Animals.*** Guard dogs and other creatures are often employed after hours to provide a layer of security. They are often perceptive and can alert anyone on the premises to an intruder. In addition, they may be dangerous enough to scare away timid burglars. They are almost always loyal and work for table scraps and scratches.

***Building.*** Simply having walls, floors, and a roof provides a business with a measure of security, especially when the business is closed to the public. Likewise, sturdy doors, gates, and windows help ensure that easy entrances can be secured. Portals that can be barred from the inside are better security than a lock in most cases.

***Guards.*** Larger businesses and those with highly valuable merchandise might employ dedicated security guards to prevent theft and deal with potentially violent patrons. Ideal guards need to both be good observers and capable of handling significant threats. Minders simply provide eyes and presence during business hours or as a watchman. A sentry makes rounds and throws out troublemakers, while a veteran guard takes measures to prevent theft, identify weaknesses, and recover lost property. See the **Hiring Costs by Skill and Proficiency** table in **Chapter 1** for costs on hiring these roles.

***Locks.*** Most locks secure potential entrances to a business while it’s closed to the public. Merchants also lock their most valuable merchandise in a cabinet to deter smash and grab theft. Simple locks are easy to defeat by anyone trained in lock picking, but they work well against casual burglars and opportunists.

***Staff.*** The first line of security for any shop is the staff. They are the shop’s eyes and ears, watching for suspicious customers and suspicious activity. Sometimes their presence and watchful eyes are enough to discourage thieves. Other times, they have to take active measures.

If the staff is confident enough, they can confront those caught in the act or kick out suspicious visitors before they steal anything. If the thieves seem too dangerous, they can call for help from a town watch or even from strangers. Unfortunately, staff are also the people in the best position to steal from a business, and shop owners can be quite wary of any new staff they don’t know and trust.

***Traps.*** Mechanical traps can stop or deter thieves, but they have drawbacks. The main issue is the safety of normal patrons. While traps can be rigged to be active or inactive, a mechanical failure can lead to disaster. In addition, most mercantile traps are not lethal, and they mark or injure a thief rather than killing them (a dead body on the premises requires an explanation). And while injuring a thief or dyeing their pants bright green might be gratifying to the owner, the business still loses coin or property.

Finally, traps can be expensive relative to the level of security they provide. The one area where they can be effective is in a deterrence if would be thieves believe there are dangerous traps in a shop. Sometimes that just means posting a sign or spreading the word.

#### Useful Security Spells

Magic offers powerful tools to both businesses and the thieves that would rob them. Just like mundane measures, it’s something of an arms race. Magic doesn’t come cheap, but a shopkeeper who practices magic has a sizable advantage in protecting goods.

***Alarm.*** *[[Rituals 1st–3rd Circle#Alarm|Alarm]]* is useful for a spellcasting shopkeeper to have a way to be alerted if someone is trying to steal from their shop. The main limitation is that a caster can only cast one at a time, so only one door, window, or 20-foot-cube area can be alarmed in this way, unless you want to pay for another caster to come that day.

***Animate Dead.*** *[[Rituals 1st–3rd Circle#Animate Dead|Animate dead]]* is an accessible spell that can create a magical guardian for a shop. Undead don’t need to sleep, eat, or take any breaks. They also don’t need to be paid. However, they usually don’t smell good, and the ethics and appearance are questionable.

***Antipathy.*** *[[Rituals 7th–9th Circle#Antipathy/Sympathy|Antipathy]]* is useful for warding a business or an area if the goal is to keep out a certain type of creature. However, it isn’t selective other than by creature type. Since most thieves resemble a business’s customers, and because the spell lasts for 10 days, it’s often not practical to stop theft. It is quite good at keeping out vermin that might damage your goods, but 8th-circle magic is overkill for combating rodents and bugs.

***Arcane Lock.*** One of the most useful security spells for any business, *[[Rituals 1st–3rd Circle#Arcane Lock|arcane lock]]* greatly enhances the value of normal physical locks. It can even be used on objects that don’t normally have a mechanical lock. Even better, you can set it so that staff can easily open whatever has been secured. While it won’t stop spellcasters who can use *[[2nd-Circle Spells#Knock|knock]]*, that’s a much rarer tool than a crowbar or a set of lockpicks.

***Divination.*** The main use of *[[Rituals 4th–6th Circle#Divination|divination]]* is to anticipate when thieves will target a business and to learn more about their plans. If cast every day, the owner is more prepared when an attempt is made.

***Forbiddance.*** This spell (*[[Rituals 4th–6th Circle#Forbiddance|forbiddance]]*) is significant for magical security because it blocks all forms of teleportation, dimensional travel, and extraplanar shenanigans that allow a spellcaster to bypass physical security and a great many magical wards as well. The problem is that it lasts only a day and costs 1,000 gp in materials. Most shopkeepers would rather get robbed than pay that kind of expense.

***Glyph of Warding.*** As one of the premier rituals used to create magical traps, *[[Rituals 1st–3rd Circle#Glyph of Warding|glyph of warding]]* is part of many a merchant’s defenses. It has an indefinite duration, can be customized to use lethal force or more humane effects, and can be keyed to trigger based on a wide set of conditions. Best of all, it isn’t especially expensive at 200 gp in materials. For most, it’s far superior to mechanical traps, which are less versatile and more costly.

***Guards and Wards.*** A *[[Rituals 4th–6th Circle#Guards and Wards|guards and wards]]* spell covers a huge area and provides layers of protection for an entire building. It’s also inexpensive, if you have access to 6th-circle spells. The limitation is that it makes exploration difficult rather than making theft difficult. For single-room shops, its effects are largely wasted.

***Locate.*** While it won’t prevent goods from being stolen, *[[Rituals 1st–3rd Circle#Locate|locate]]* is handy for recovering them.

***Message.*** The *[[Cantrips#Message|message]]* spell can be extremely useful to summon authorities to a shop quickly when robbers arrive.

***Private Sanctuary.*** *Private sanctuary* is a significant spell for magical security because it blocks teleportation and planar travel, two effects that bypass almost all physical security. Not only that, but it can block divination, protecting the secrets and defenses of any secure location. Best of all, it can be made permanent after a year of applying it to the same location.

***Symbol.*** *[[Rituals 7th–9th Circle#Symbol|Symbol]]* is a spell for creating magical traps. It has most of the same advantages as *[[Rituals 1st–3rd Circle#Glyph of Warding|glyph of warding]]*. While not quite as versatile, it offers some powerful effects, including the potential of instant death for an intruder. It’s also somewhat more difficult to detect and remove. On the other hand, it’s very expensive to cast and in the 7th circle, a rarified sort of magic that isn’t easy to contract for.
