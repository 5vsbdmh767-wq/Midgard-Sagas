All these places are thought to be legends, and some are said to be no more than a skald's fancy. None is easily found on a map, but surely some are real, and could be found by a bold explorer.

## Ansaroth

North of Reykurbrand lie the ruins of Ansaroth, ancient beyond memory. No record of its original purpose exists. Most of the site is underground; on the surface stand newer, cruder buildings. About a hundred years ago, thuellai escorted a force of trolls, bugbears, trollkin, and fraughashar there in the service of [[Boreas (North Wind)|Boreas]], for reasons he never gave. Their descendants, the Ansarothr, still hold the ruin. They force captives to explore the labyrinth below and violently drive off outsiders. Hardy as they are, they consider the ruins below cursed and haunted, and only the worst winter weather or the harshest punishment drives them down. Dwarven stonework marks the passages near the surface; the deeper chambers bear unsettling images and alien glyphs.

Above ground, trollkin warbands of about thirty live in longhouses, each led by a mated pair of warriors or a troll warlord, and fraughashar live in log cabins with beds stacked like cordwood, each family sheltered by as many bugbears as it can stand. The two peoples keep apart and feud over resources. At the center, all that remains of the original surface ruin is a stone floor and six feet of a thick defensive wall, beside many holes leading down. The largest, a crevasse called the Darkening Step, has the only circular stair of dwarven work, and every opening is heavily guarded. Over the old floor stands Rimespike Vault, a thatched roof on four pillars of bundled mammoth bone, where the Ansarothr punish those who break the laws of Boreas and the community. Minor crimes earn flogging. Serious offenders are marched below with a modest weapon and told not to come back for five nights or be killed on sight. None has ever returned.

Ansarothr raiders range far to take captives or to trade with giantkin villages in [[Jotunheim]]. Captives languish in guarded stockades, starved and abused, and the unlucky are forced below to explore the Darkness Beyond Torchlight: halls and chambers whose purposes are lost, wider near the top and narrowing farther down, full of aberrations and other horrors, with traps that reset themselves and every kind of terrifying sight and sound. Groups as large as a dozen warriors have vanished there. The most successful and longest-lived of the forced explorers, the dwarf Arnka Shuthamar, says the rooms taper toward a single spiral stair cut into the inside of an enormous shaft she calls the Descent. Its walls are carved into titanic statues of humanoids with strange, alien faces, and its steps go down several hundred feet before breaking off; the shaft goes deeper, at least as far as a dropped torch can show. The Ansarothr keep her apart from the other prisoners, sure she isn't telling them everything.

The fraughashar priest of Boreas Kissan Virnstyr controls Ansaroth's bugbears with fey guile and frost bolts, and lately the hunters struggle to find enough fresh meat for them. Blarban Eye-Eater, king of the trolls and trollkin, has his own trouble: for months, trolls have been waking covered in blood from wounds already healed, with no memory of being attacked, and many now question his leadership.

## Grokeheim, the Snow Queen's Palace

Delicate spires of ice pierce the horizon like frosty spears. Grokeheim is the realm of Her Crystalline Majesty, the Snow Queen Morrinn, daughter of [[Boreas (North Wind)|Boreas]]. Each morning, as dawn touches its towers, the castle moves itself to a new glacier or stretch of frozen tundra on [[The Bleak Expanse]], wherever the Snow Queen pleases, and it is never found in the same place twice. Whispers say she could make it appear anywhere in the North, but simply hasn't chosen to yet. Its beauty, and hers, lure lost and foolish travelers to its gates, where her servants meet them. Those who survive learn that the Mistress of Grokeheim is much like her realm: beautiful, cold, and unforgivingly deadly. Few remember a time when Grokeheim was not at the edge of their fears. Many say Boreas gave it to his only daughter out of benevolence; others, that he was jealous of a southern god who gave his own daughter a whole duchy. Bolder voices say it shows the limits of the North Wind's power, since his gift never seems to pass beyond the glaciers.

Deep snow that slows everyone but Morrinn's ice maidens covers its grounds. Within, floors and walls of blue ice trap sunlight and release it at odd hours, so the castle is always dimly lit, even at night, and always bitterly cold, except in the rare moments when a visitor makes the Snow Queen laugh. Fey ice maidens, fraughashar, ogres, frost giants, thursir, and trollkin people its towers, halls, barracks, and libraries. Morrinn changes its layout on a whim, but a few rooms stay put.

Adventurers do seek Grokeheim, usually to ransom a prisoner, find magic recovered from the wastes, or learn something only its library holds. They are often surprised that the Snow Queen wants payment in service, not coin. Her court has puzzles of its own. Her newest ladies-in-waiting are three strange mortals, Rella, a dwarven tinker; Lumibelle, a human sailor; and Sinikka, a trollkin vintner, oddities in a palace with no gears, ships, or grapes, who clearly have the queen's favor. And for six months, on the morning after every full moon, someone has left butchered game in plain sight of the south tower, and her ice maidens have failed to learn who.

## Hyperborea

Stories say that beneath the top of the world, where the polar lights dance, the hidden realm of Hyperborea bursts with life, and natural law bends and mingles with the unbounded energies of the Outer Planes. Far beyond the maps, where the sun rises and sets once a year, a circle of rune-carved granite wall ten miles high rises from a caldera in the Lung of the Sea. Four great barbicans break it, each sealed with a priceless gate of prismatic steel big enough to swallow two longships sailing abreast. The gates have not opened in a thousand years and lie under layer upon layer of ice. Within, the air is as warm as a southern desert, jungle grows from swampy volcanic earth, and the mist of hot springs drifts among trees and tiny flowers found nowhere else. At its center, on the Thulang Plateau, lie seven silent cities whose basalt crypts and tombs are choked with the treasures and horrors of a primordial age. Few reach them, and fewer survive.

Hyperborea appeared in the far north between two and three thousand years ago, and giants and gods alike found its wall impervious. Its wizards later aided the Vanguard Kingdoms. Hyperborean ruins still dot the tundra of [[Thursrike]].

### The Drownstone Road

The halls of the ancient dwarven empire sank beneath the waves, but they are not empty. Since the Reaving, dwarven explorers have returned with stories of half-flooded passages and damp chambers that survived the deluge, home to degenerate survivors and nameless things that came up from the seabed into the drowned mines. This network of dripping tunnels deep underground, the Drownstone Road, runs the whole width of the Nieder Strait. Few dare it: the endless dark, the weight of water overhead, and the piping and howling of lurking aberrations are enough to break the stoutest dwarf.

## Nordheim and the Fallen Kingdoms

Long ago, the dwarven holds of Aurvang and Nordheim allied with Issedon, a stronghold of warriors from what is now [[Trollheim]] and powerful wizards from Hyperborea. These Vanguard Kingdoms guarded the world against the rage of Boreas, and their greatest work was the Wall, a line of magically fortified bulwarks sealing the mountain passes north of Trollheim, [[Huldramose]], and [[Wolfheim]]. For centuries dwarven masonry and Hyperborean magic held back Boreas's thuellai, frost giants, and ice elementals. Then Boreas loosed the living glaciers: masses of ice larger than kingdoms, creeping slowly and unstoppably south, crushing everything before them. The North was once home to greater wonders still, the rainbow bridge Bifrost to the realm of the gods, the Godshall of Valhalla, the battleground of the Plane of Spears, and the Storm Court of [[Perun]], but those gateways were stolen by the elves and moved, some say to [[Valera]], and then shut entirely.

### Nordheim

The ruins of Nordheim lie in a wide valley between five realms: Wolfheim to the south, Huldramose to the southwest, Trollheim to the east, Jotunheim to the west, and the Bleak Expanse to the north. It was a fortress-city of towering rune-carved walls in the foothills, with streets wide enough for a marching phalanx, reinforced gate towers, and trained giant eagles patrolling the skies, and beneath it miles of tunnels, galleries, workshops, temples, and homes. The greatest of the dwarven holds after the Reaving, it was also the first to fall, and the dwarves never learned how. "Never by siege," they say with fierce certainty. About a thousand years ago, in a terrible winter that closed the roads, something crept up from the depths and put out its hearths and forge fires, destroying the city from within. The few who escaped lived on its outskirts, too far away to understand. [[Dwarves]] blame frost giants and their jotun lords, Boreas and his winds, fey conspiracies and elven ghosts, even demons from beyond the endless void of [[Ginnungagap]]. Most scholars blame Boreas, conspiring with Chernobog to break one Vanguard Kingdom and so doom the rest. A group of dwarven scholars in [[Skaldholm]] proposed that Nordheim's reavers took their holy fervor too far, sacrificing thinking beings and angering [[Thor]]; their work was quickly discredited and suppressed, and one of them who went into Nordheim to learn more vanished.

For nearly fifty years, ever-larger forces failed to retake the city, and none returned. Then the glaciers breached the Wall. When Boreas's armies broke through to raze Aurvang and Issedon, a force twice as large went to Nordheim and spent two years burying the city under earth and stone. Only a quarter of the giants, elementals, and ice maidens sent there survived the task.

Today, a few crumbling tower tops and scorched blocks of wall poke out of the ground. In a deep pit on the site's south side is a desecrated temple of [[Sif]], and from it shuffling lines of undead dig tirelessly to uncover more of the city. Around the ruins lies a wide, nearly silent land without birds or beasts, which migrating herds go out of their way to avoid. Instead, the ruins and the forest around them crawl with restless dwarven corpses, the murmuring dead, who whisper and babble endlessly of who they were, as if trying to remember forgotten glories. Now and then they can understand and even be reasoned with, but those moments are rare, and whatever killed the dwarves still lingers in them, driving them to prey on the minds and souls of anyone who crosses their land. Thousands walk between the river and the old borders of Nordheim. Their range matches the city's underground borders, and the priests of [[Wotan]] report that this is also a line the Valkyries are forbidden to cross. Some years ago, thralls sent north from Wolfheim to learn why the forest animals had fled found the pit and the temple, and a sizable force of walking dwarven corpses followed them silently to the edge of the forest before fading back into the trees.

Legends speak of wonders still buried there. The Court of the Lost Bifrost was the council chamber of Nordheim's High King, where nobles sat in an obsidian gallery around the Skurdr Throne and the rainbow bridge itself rose from the center of the oval court, until elven archmages stole it shortly before the city's fall; the gods later took it back, and it now falls where they will. Its mark lingers, thinning the walls between worlds. The Tholos of Nordheim, the city's greatest temple, lies deep beneath it, hewn from gold-veined marble, with a flickering portal between altars to Thor and [[Volund]] that once linked it to the court, guarded by iron golems and vaettir priests and mages. And at the city's heart, behind concentric trap-filled corridors that turn against one another, lies the adamantine Vault of the High King. If fragments of letters from the Vanguard Kingdoms can be believed, it still holds the legendary Axe of Forseti and a crown set with a piece of the Dominion Orb.

### Aurvang

On the shores of the Tearstain River stand the ruins of Aurvang, once a dwarfhold of deep ring magic and a place of pilgrimage, where the Eye of Wotan and the Graven Shield of the dwarf maiden Grajvar were kept. It was famous for hard work, bitter strength of arms, and ruthless merchants. Its last king, Regni Othinson, tried to bargain with a red dragon to protect the city from Boreas and came up short. The wyrm still lingers in the ruins, and fiery wyrmlings and linnorms hunt reindeer, elk, and people in the valley. Nothing has thrived there since dwarven days, but its treasure halls are rumored to stand intact somewhere behind a stone door.

### Issedon

Issedon's ruins lie southwest of Aurvang, frozen in a blue-white glacier half a mile thick. The ice never melts, yet stops at the city walls, and through it broken battlements stretch toward the surface. A few give a way in to adventurers with ice-breaching spells or the nerve to brave remorhaz tunnels. Some of Issedon's people survived its fall, entombed, and their depraved descendants still scrabble through its tunnels in an instinctive hunt for treasure, stealing worthless junk and priceless relics from one another, distracted only by fresh meat. The undead remnants of Issedon's defenders, ghosts and vaettir commanded by a cabal of liches, still stand against the North Wind in the passes, and do not suffer interference or suggestions that their troops move on to their rest. An Issedonian tower buried in the glacier has lately been exposed along the shore of Trollheim.

### The Falling Wall

When Nordheim fell, the Vanguard Kingdoms fell to the primordial ice, the Wall ruptured, and Aurvang and Issedon were laid waste. Though collapsed in many places, the stones and sorcery of the Wall, now called the Falling Wall, still hold back the onslaught of Boreas and his creatures, but every year another section topples and lets more of his power into the world.

## Reykurbrand, the Hall of Surtr

Reykurbrand is the stronghold of Surtr and Sinmara, a married pair of ancient jotun, grandchildren of Aurgelmir and among the very few who survived the destruction of the First World. The name belongs both to a titanic, solitary volcano north of [[Thrumagard]] and to the jotun-sized fortress ringing its caldera. Its warmth keeps the ground nearby clear of ice. The towers of Surtr's hall spear the sky, its obsidian ramparts glow with the magma below, and the whole keep is forever shrouded in volcanic smoke. A small army of fire giants guards it against the gods, some of whom foolishly believe that killing Surtr will save them from their fate.

The throne room's open outer wall overlooks the churning caldera, which hides a portal to Muspelheim, the plane of fire. Its only furnishings are twin thrones and a pair of obsidian crowns hanging above a chest of solid diorite. When Ragnarok comes, the skalds say, Surtr and Sinmara will put on their crowns, Surtr will draw his burning blade Jordbani, the "World Slayer," from the chest, and together they will set the world ablaze.

After the first war with the gods, Reykurbrand was a nerve center where the jotun plotted against their enemies, and impossible weapons, forbidden rituals, and terrible creatures were born in its magma. Over the centuries, though, Surtr and Sinmara let go of vengeance and broke with the other giants. Those who held to revenge left to settle [[Sengajordensblod]], and Reykurbrand became a refuge for the pair's fire giant descendants, for other giants who had given up their grudges, and for any curiosity or outcast who caught their eye. The two accept that Ragnarok is inevitable and that Surtr will one day draw his sword, but they think the world as it is worth remembering and enjoying. Surtr wanders the lands to see and experience as much as he can, leaving Jordbani in Sinmara's care, and is said to sometimes carry home interesting people so their life stories can be recorded. Sinmara gathers the greatest treasures of the doomed world into a memorial, by trade or by fire giant reavers, unconcerned with what their former owners think, in the hope they will survive Ragnarok and be found.

## The Shards of Aurgelmir

When the gods killed Aurgelmir, they destroyed the First World he had made, plucked it from the World Tree, and hung a new world carved from his corpse in its place. That vast making and unmaking was not seamless, and its scars remain. Some are tiny: a rock around which water never freezes, a razor-sharp shard of glass that can't cut flesh, a cave that gives anyone who sleeps there nightmares of an empty, ever-changing Other. Others are huge: whole plateaus under a bleeding red sky where humanoids never became the dominant life, and spiders, squid, or stranger things built civilizations. These are the Shards of Aurgelmir, pieces of Aurgelmir or his First World, fundamentally alien places where the rules of reality, magic, and life work differently and the walls between the planes run thin or vanish. Mortals can't live in them long, and even visiting is beyond reckless, but they hold wonders and power that constantly tempt mortals in.

## The Shoals of the Selkies

South of [[Huldramose]] and [[Hrafnheim]], razor shoals surround what were once great mountaintops. They tear the bottoms out of ships and dash their crews on the rocks, and the shallows are littered with the dead, yet more sailors keep coming for the wisdom of the Skandersteinn, treasures from before the Reaving hidden in the island caves, and selkie thralls for their crews. These were once among the most peaceful lands in the North: quiet and rural even by Northern standards, with no towns, only tiny fishing villages and mountainside homesteads. Shoal-born navigators are famous across the North and are said to be able to sail a fishing boat through a raging squall without going off course. Fishing captains fish alongside seals, sharing their catch for schools herded into their nets; others guide travelers through the shoals for luxuries and loot, or dive for treasure in lost wrecks.

The selkies live hidden among the islanders, and most families here claim some selkie blood. Anyone in the Shoals might be one. The selkies reveal themselves only to drive off threats, leading herds of seals to crack open raiders' hulls, drown their crews, and spread their cargo ashore. Once a year, the selkies and the other seafolk of the Nieder Strait gather for a great althing around the Skandersteinn.

The selkies settled the shoals after the Reaving, when the flood swallowed their island homes, and dragged lost treasures up from the depths to the new peaks. Mortals came later: lost traders washed ashore, and the beloved of selkies and other sea folk, who gave up everything they knew rather than lose them. In time the mortals outnumbered the selkies, who vanished into their numbers.

Over the last five years, raiders from Trollheim and [[Stannasgard]] have attacked the isles. The first came for the wisdom of the Skandersteinn and, when refused, brought huge raiding parties to take what they wanted. Most died, but the glory and treasure of the survivors drew wave after wave of imitators seeking treasure, captives, navigators, and even selkies. For a time the raids overwhelmed the defenders; the islanders learned to flee their villages at the first sight of a sail. Now the enraged selkies attack passing ships without discrimination and lure them onto the rocks. Bjorg Thrice-Burnt, a strong-bodied human mage with webbed fingers and burn scars across her lower body, a skilled diver and master of water-breathing magic who plays up rumors that she is a selkie, is one of the few natives still willing to trust outsiders. She fears the selkies' attacks will bring the Northern kingdoms' wrath down on the isles, and that the surface folk, who can't flee into the sea, will pay for it.

### The Skandersteinn

At the very top of each Selkie Isle stands a runed stone obelisk twice a man's height, humming a low drone that sharpens into an endless chant if one listens closely. These are the Skandersteinn, made by Vaknir Grathegn, High King of the fallen dwarven empire, to record his glory forever, and hauled by selkies from his sunken palace. Rumors of their powers run across the North: that they show the single greatest glory you will ever achieve, that they know all the secrets of the North's past and reveal them to the worthy, that they grant power to the faithful. The few allowed near them tell wild, contradictory stories: their pasts dragged to the surface, their worst fears scoured away, the exact moment of their deaths revealed, their enemies' secrets ringing in their heads. They are the center of religious and political life in the Shoals. Althings meet around them, a holmganga is only lawful within sight of one, and great rites expose petitioners to a carefully measured taste of their power. The selkies guard them above everything, even letting reavers attack their families or steal their sealskins if it means fending off an attack on the stones.

## The Vaettir Mounds

Burial mounds are scattered all across the North. The smallest are mere rises where poor warriors lie with their weapons and favorite things; the grandest are great hills holding dozens of dead and hoards of treasure, and the most renowned heroes and leaders may be buried with their longships. The dead keep their most valuable treasures so they will be ready when Ragnarok comes. Many mounds have a shrine alcove in the side where families leave offerings and pray for guidance and protection. Along the [[Northlands|Reaver Coast]], many barrows double as beacons against attack, and locals honor the dead with prayers and gifts in exchange for an undead warrior or three waiting to greet raiders.

Sometimes the dead do not rest. Grave robbers or monsters disturb them, or restless spirits come back to finish what they left undone. Vaettir who rise to protect their communities, train new warriors, or share the wisdom of past generations are a blessing. Those woken by robbers or monsters unleash their rage on whole regions.

Many vaettir were warriors whose deaths didn't earn them Valhalla, sent back to their mounds for a second chance at a glorious end; some set out at once to find it, and others stand watch over the communities they loved, sure that battle will find them. Others rise from wealth, cowardice, and unpleasantness: people rich enough never to risk a warrior's death, vain enough to demand honor they never earned, and given a warrior's funeral anyway, whom even Hel holds in contempt and sends back until they meet a death fit for their burial. Most of these care more about regaining their old wealth and prestige than earning a better death. The sokknair, the "sunken dead," were buried in lands now under the Nieder Strait and the Swive, where the floods smashed their mounds and scattered them across the depths. Many woke from that sacrilege and now pull themselves onto anchored ships or into fishing nets; those who keep their minds want fitting second deaths and burial on dry land. Rarest are the witch-vaettir, spellcasters who plan their return in advance, layering their graves with magic, carving runes into their own living bones, and even arranging their deaths; some become runewights or liches, and they often gather entourages of other vaettir before revealing themselves.

More and more vaettir have been waking lately. Poorer settlements suffer vengeful dead, would-be protectors who drain what little wealth they have, and feuding vaettir who each demand the locals' loyalty. The [[Cult of Ragnarok]] calls it a sign that Ragnarok is so near the dead rise to meet it, though most vaettir, even the foulest witch-vaettir, have shown no interest in the cult. The bearfolk skalds of [[Björnrike]] are more worried. They believe an intelligence is behind it, and suspect the ancient vaettir Ottar Harjaz.

| Skarde the Reaver | A famous pirate who died of illness coming home from a failed expedition, so loved by his crew that they buried him with his longship on the Wolfheim coast. Now a vaettir, he wants only to die in a worthy duel, but his strength and skill have felled every challenger, so he pays passing skalds with treasure from his mound to spread word that he needs a proper foe. |
