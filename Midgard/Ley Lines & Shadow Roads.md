Ley lines are invisible rivers of magic running through the land. Some call them the scales of Veles or the bones of the world. They strengthen spells, arcane and divine alike, and they feed the old wards, the spells of liches, and the shadow roads. An ordinary eye can't see them, but a trained spellcaster sees them as glowing threads or bands of light. They aren't everywhere. The [[Western Wastes]] have almost none, and they're rare underground.

Weak ley lines turn up nearly anywhere: along streambeds, at standing stones, old trees, bridges, and crossroads. They vanish from consecrated temples and freshly plowed fields, though they come back in time. Strong lines run through places with a magical reputation, like hilltops, river forks, stone circles, druid groves, and elven ruins. Titanic lines are rare. They sit under great temples, mountain peaks, volcanoes, and the hearts of glaciers, and elder dragons and greater demons like to make their lairs on them.

##### The Shadow Roads
The elves called them the fey roads, and [[Grand Duchy of Dornig|Dornig]] still does. They are paths through [[The Shadow Realm]], powered by the strongest ley lines, that can carry a traveler hundreds of miles in a few days. The elves cut them long ago, driving back the beasts of Shadow and building walls and mithral bridges along the way. When the [[Shadow Fey|shadow fey]] made their bargain with [[Sarastra]], the roads were split between the summer elves and those of Shadow, and the shadow roads have only multiplied since.

A shadow road has fixed gates at each end, and a gate seldom stays open for long. Opening one usually takes a spell, a passphrase, or a fey guide, and elves and the elfmarked have an easier time of it than anyone else. Inside, the roads are hedge mazes, twisting tunnels, and hollows, rarely a straight path. Anyone who leaves the path loses whatever protection the road's masters offer and may take days to find their way back. Fey, undead, and shadow beasts hunt the roads, and the shadow fey keep black stone keeps along some of them to collect tolls.
